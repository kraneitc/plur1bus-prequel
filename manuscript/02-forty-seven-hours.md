# PART TWO

## FORTY-SEVEN HOURS

Eight months later, the most expensive nothing in New Mexico needed its cage changed.

Jenn marked the rat’s card, transferred the animal into a clean enclosure, and wrote **NO CLINICAL SIGNS** for the two hundred and twelfth time.

The words had acquired a rhythm independent of meaning. Weight normal. Coat normal. Feeding normal. Social behavior normal. No lesions. No discharge. No tremor. No aggression. No evidence that a molecular sentence broadcast from six hundred light-years away had done anything more consequential than complicate her shift schedule.

Upstairs, people called it the Sequence. Down here it was why the ventilated rack beeped whenever somebody used the microwave.

Mel Ortega leaned into the animal-room vestibule holding two pink boxes.

“Peace offering.”

“For what?” Jenn asked through her respirator.

“Existing.”

“Not accepted.”

“Then fundraiser. Sofia’s band needs uniforms.”

He opened one box under the observation window. Glazed doughnuts shone in two rows. One had lost a patch of icing to the lid.

“What does she play?” Jenn asked.

“Trombone.”

“On purpose?”

Mel tapped the box. A cartoon trombone wearing marching shoes had been drawn on the label in purple marker. “Fourteen-year-old girls do nothing accidentally.”

Deshpande arrived behind him and inspected the doughnuts with greater interest than he had shown the rats all week.

“Those cannot come inside containment.”

“They’re for outside containment,” Mel said.

“Then why are you holding them inside the vestibule?”

Mel looked down at the line painted on the floor. His left shoe had crossed it.

“History will forgive me.”

“History has paperwork.”

Jenn laughed hard enough to fog her face shield.

At 3:21 a.m., she and Deshpande began the final euthanasia run.

The project had moved through macaques, rabbits, mice, and guinea pigs without a phenotype. Rats were meant to settle the matter before funding review. The official hypothesis was that S-78 either could not complete its life cycle or produced no observable effect. The unofficial hypothesis was that everybody had wasted a year.

Jenn hated gassing animals. Deshpande hated cardiac puncture. They chose cardiac puncture because seniority was useful mostly as a way of assigning yourself the task you disliked less.

Rat 4C lay on its side beneath the bedding.

“Already dead?” Jenn asked.

Deshpande lifted it by the base of the tail. It hung without resistance.

He pressed two fingers against its chest, then changed fingers as if the first pair might be mistaken.

“Strong heartbeat.”

“How strong?”

“As in not subtle.”

Jenn leaned close. The rat’s black eye reflected the white room without responding to it.

“Catatonia?”

“In a rat?”

“You asked me.”

“I did not.”

The rat moved when she reached for it.

The bite entered at the side of her index finger, through glove and skin. Pain arrived as a clean electrical fact. She jerked back. The rat struck the table, righted itself, then became still again.

Deshpande said a word his grant officer would not have approved.

Jenn stripped the outer glove. Blood welled through a crescent no longer than a grain of rice.

“Time,” Deshpande said.

She looked at the wall clock. “Three thirty-six.”

They performed the protocol exactly.

Water first. Encourage bleeding. Antiseptic. Secure the animal. Notify biosafety. Photograph the wound. Bag the glove. Record lot numbers. Do not allow fear to improvise improvements.

At 3:43, Jenn’s teeth began to chatter.

“Cold?” Deshpande asked.

She said no, but her jaw said it badly.

The tremor moved into her shoulders, hands, abdomen, legs. It was not a seizure. She remained conscious, which meant she experienced every muscle attempting to follow instructions issued from nowhere. Her breath shortened. Deshpande guided her to the floor before her knees failed.

“Jenn. Look at me.”

“I am.”

“Tell me your full name.”

She did.

“Where are you?”

“At work, regretting my career choices.”

“Pupils equal,” he said, more to the room than to her.

The episode lasted ninety-four seconds.

By the time occupational health arrived, Jenn was sitting against the cabinet and objecting to the word *episode*.

“An episode implies content,” she said. “That was a malfunction.”

They placed her in negative-pressure observation anyway.

* * *

At hour six, Mara flew in from Washington.

She entered Jenn’s room wearing a powered respirator and carrying a tablet inside two plastic sleeves. Her blue suit was visible beneath the gown.

“You look like a very formal beekeeper,” Jenn said.

“How do you feel?”

“Like everyone keeps asking me how I feel.”

“Headache?”

“No.”

“Metallic taste?”

“No.”

“Thirst?”

Jenn looked at the empty water pitcher. “That proves I drank water.”

“It proves you requested three liters in six hours.”

“The air is dry.”

Mara recorded the answer without endorsing it.

Blood PCR was negative. The sample from the bite was negative. Saliva was indeterminate, then weakly positive on an exploratory assay that had never been validated for diagnosis.

Mara ordered the result repeated with independent controls. She asked the genomics unit to begin an antisense line without waiting for certainty; the target design stayed inside that team.

Deshpande stood beside her at the observation glass.

“And where would it have to work?” he asked.

“First we need a molecule.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the answer that comes before the answer.”

“You sound pleased.”

Mara turned on him. “I sound busy.”

“You were right. It did something.”

“A person was injured.”

“Both facts can exist.”

Jenn watched them through the glass. She could not hear the words, but she knew when Mara said *injured*. The knowledge arrived before Mara’s lips formed the second syllable.

Jenn sat up.

Mara looked at her.

For an instant Jenn experienced the room from the other side of the glass: her own small body on the bed, the pitcher, the pulse-oximeter wire, Deshpande’s reflection superimposed across her face.

Then she was back inside herself, gripping the blanket.

“I need you in here,” she said through the intercom.

“Why?” Mara asked.

Jenn almost told her.

Instead she said, “Because I can’t smell the alcohol wipe.”

* * *

At hour nineteen, Jenn failed six smell tests and passed twelve she invented to prove the first six unfair.

At hour twenty-six, Deshpande entered with a paper cup and stopped inside the door.

“You found RNA in the saliva,” Jenn said.

He had not spoken.

His face changed.

The room changed with it—not visually, but in the way a room changes when someone behind you raises a hand.

“Mara told you?”

“No.”

“You saw the result?”

“No.”

“Then why did you say it?”

Jenn considered lying. The impulse produced a peculiar pressure behind her eyes, as if another person had put a thumb against the thought.

“Because you came in holding water instead of coffee,” she said. “And you only do that when you’re trying to look calm.”

It was plausible. It was also not why.

Deshpande accepted the explanation provisionally. That hurt more than suspicion would have.

At hour thirty-one, she dreamed of fluorescent light through the eyes of the rat.

There was no language in the dream. No cage, because a cage required a distinction between inside and outside that the animal did not possess. There was warmth, food, the pressure of nearby bodies, and a vast repeating absence every seventy-eight seconds. Not a sound. A place where something should be.

She woke with her bitten finger in her mouth.

* * *

Arlo Baines cleaned the laboratory floors at night because machines objected less than people when he listened to baseball.

At hour thirty-seven, his buffer drew a strip of disposable gown beneath its housing and locked the drive wheel. Arlo unplugged it, knelt, and reached farther under the deck than the safety sticker recommended.

The metal edge cut the web between his thumb and forefinger.

Jenn knew before the radio call.

She was standing at the observation-room door when Mel brought Arlo into the corridor.

“How did you—” Mel began.

“I heard him,” Jenn said.

Arlo held his bleeding hand above his heart. “I didn’t yell.”

Jenn opened the first-aid cabinet. She was still technically confined, but the door had been left unsecured after twelve hours of normal vitals. She irrigated the cut, patted it dry, and reached for gauze.

Her glove tore on the foil packet.

She had been biting the inside of her cheek all night. A pinpoint of blood and saliva marked the gauze when she held one end between her lips to tear it.

Arlo saw it.

Jenn saw him see it.

They both looked at the open cut.

“New gauze,” she said.

She dropped the contaminated square into the red bin.

Later, frame-by-frame review would show that the corner brushed Arlo’s wound as it fell. At the time, nobody noticed.

At hour forty, Jenn felt panic beneath the floor.

It was not hers. Hers arrived a moment later in response.

She walked to the observation-room wall and put her palm against it. Two floors below, Arlo sat in occupational health with a normal temperature and a heart rate of one hundred thirty.

Jenn slowed her breathing.

The other panic altered. Not diminished exactly. It found the edges of her rhythm and began to follow.

She tried the sequence again: four counts in, six out. Four in, six out.

Arlo’s monitor slowed.

When Mara came in, Jenn did not mention him.

That was the choice.

Reporting the correlation would separate them into different facilities. Mara would call it containment. Jenn imagined Arlo alone with an impossible fear that was partly hers and decided that observation could wait until morning.

She asked for an EEG instead.

“Why?” Mara said.

“Because something happened.”

“What happened?”

Jenn felt Arlo waiting through concrete and ductwork. She could not hear a sentence. She knew only that another person was listening for her answer.

“I knew something before I knew it,” she said.

Mara’s stillness was total.

“What?”

“That’s the problem. It wasn’t a fact. It was a person.”

* * *

At hour forty-seven, two EEG machines recorded in two rooms on two floors.

Mara synchronized their clocks against the same local standard, then deliberately disconnected the network cable between the acquisition systems. She made Mel confirm the physical disconnect. Deshpande inspected both electrode maps. Arlo’s wound was swabbed. Jenn’s saliva was collected again. Every step received a time, an initial, and a second witness.

“We’re going to open and close our eyes on instruction,” Mara said over the intercom. “Nothing else.”

Jenn lay still beneath the cap.

The first trial showed nothing.

The second showed artifact from Arlo swallowing.

On the third, Jenn felt the approaching gap—the absence from the rat’s dream, returning on schedule. She opened her eyes before Mara gave the instruction.

Two floors below, Arlo opened his.

Both heart rates descended.

Not identically. One followed the other by a fraction of a second, corrected, overshot, and settled into the same repeating variation.

Mara stared at the traces.

Jenn felt her astonishment arrive through Arlo, who could not see Mara at all.

For the first time since the bite, Jenn was certain she was not alone.

The certainty did not comfort her.
