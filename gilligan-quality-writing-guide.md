# The Gilligan Quality Framework

## A practical story guide for *Pluribus: Before We Were Us*

This is not a guide to copying Vince Gilligan's voice, dialogue, shots, or surface mannerisms. It extracts durable craft principles from his work and turns them into an original development process for this prequel.

The target is not “make it feel like *Breaking Bad*.” The target is the deeper standard shared by *The X-Files*, *Breaking Bad*, *Better Call Saul*, *El Camino*, and *Pluribus*: a bold premise made credible by human behavior, patient observation, exact causality, visual invention, moral consequence, and tonal confidence.

---

## 1. The core model

Gilligan's strongest stories can be reduced to one dramatic chain:

> **A flawed person faces a practical problem, chooses a defensible solution for a compromised reason, succeeds just enough to create a worse problem, and must live with the consequence.**

For this series:

> **People repeatedly choose care, knowledge, safety, or order; each choice works locally; together those successes abolish private humanity.**

That second sentence is the creative constitution of *Before We Were Us*. Every major beat should prove it.

### The governing loop

Use this loop at season, episode, sequence, and scene scale:

1. **Want** — What does the character need right now, in concrete terms?
2. **Pressure** — Why must they act before they are emotionally or factually ready?
3. **Choice** — What do they freely decide? Avoid accidents doing the decisive moral work.
4. **Method** — What physical process do they use to pursue the choice?
5. **Success** — Let the method work, at least partly.
6. **Residue** — What new evidence, obligation, lie, injury, witness, or relationship remains?
7. **Consequence** — How does that residue become the next problem?
8. **Change** — What can the character no longer believe about themself?

If two consecutive beats are connected only by “and then,” the chain is weak. Prefer **therefore**, **but**, or **because**.

## 2. Ten craft principles

### 1. Build from a moral experiment, not a message

The premise should be expressible as a transformation or contradiction, not an argument the show intends to win.

- *Breaking Bad*: what if a frustrated, capable man discovers that transgression makes him feel alive?
- *Better Call Saul*: what if love and recognition bring out both the best and worst in a gifted corner-cutter?
- *Pluribus*: what if universal happiness and kindness become a threat to human freedom?
- *Before We Were Us*: what if every step that ends individual humanity is taken by people trying to reduce harm?

Do not make characters spokespersons for the theme. Give each one a need that makes one side of the theme feel personally necessary.

**Test:** Can the episode make two incompatible actions feel humane?

### 2. Character creates plot

Plot turns should come from a person's established competence, blind spot, appetite, fear, or loyalty. The writers may know the destination; the character must still build the road.

For every major turn, complete this sentence:

> Only **this character**, because of **this trait/history**, would choose **this method**, producing **this consequence**.

If any competent scientist, soldier, or official could perform the beat unchanged, it is plot delivery rather than character drama.

### 3. Actions keep accruing interest

Gilligan has repeatedly described *Breaking Bad* and *Better Call Saul* as stories in which actions have consequences. Consequence is not merely punishment. It is continuity with memory.

Track four kinds of residue:

- **Material:** samples, footage, injuries, access logs, contaminated food, broken equipment.
- **Institutional:** reports, authorizations, classifications, jurisdiction, command precedent.
- **Relational:** suspicion, debt, shame, gratitude, betrayed confidence.
- **Interior:** a line crossed, a rationalization adopted, a fear confirmed.

Nothing important resets between episodes. An apparently minor residue should be allowed to mature into a major turn.

### 4. Make intelligence increase difficulty

Characters should rarely miss the obvious merely to preserve suspense. Let them notice, investigate, adapt, and make sensible countermoves. Drama comes from intelligent people having incomplete information, conflicting duties, or incompatible definitions of harm.

In this prequel:

- Mara understands the biology but misreads consent.
- Rios understands adversarial systems but misreads benevolence.
- Leah understands wellness but may misread personhood.
- The Joined understand nearly everyone but cannot understand the value of an inviolable boundary.

Each is formidable inside their frame and dangerous outside it.

### 5. Give the impossible one miracle; audit everything around it

Gilligan's speculative work gains force from ordinary reality behaving precisely. Establish one irreducible impossibility, then make logistics, institutions, biology, travel time, injuries, and human error obey rules.

For *Before We Were Us*, the miracle is remote transfer of memory and expertise through the S-78 field effect. It should not also provide convenient transmission, perfect prediction, instant access to unobserved facts, or selective exceptions.

Maintain a **rule ledger** with:

- what the phenomenon can do;
- what it cannot do;
- what characters currently believe;
- what the audience currently knows;
- what each new revelation costs to demonstrate.

### 6. Turn process into suspense

The camera should not merely observe a result; it should make the audience understand a procedure well enough to fear one deviation.

A process sequence needs:

1. a legible goal;
2. an ordered set of actions;
3. a constraint or clock;
4. one meaningful unknown;
5. a change in the pattern;
6. a result with residue.

Patient pace is earned by changing the viewer's understanding during the procedure. Duration without accumulating meaning is not suspense.

### 7. Write images before explanations

Gilligan has said that he needs to “watch the show” in his head and that his rooms repeatedly ask what the audience sees first in each scene. Treat the screenplay as cinema, not illustrated dialogue.

For every scene, specify:

- the first image;
- the dominant physical action;
- the object or spatial relation carrying the subtext;
- the visual turn;
- the last image.

Remove dialogue that merely names what the image already proves.

Objects should do at least two jobs. A doughnut can be workplace banality, a character detail, a transmission vehicle, and a later source of guilt. If it serves only as a symbol, it will feel arranged.

### 8. Let point of view shape sympathy—but audit the distortion

Gilligan has acknowledged that *Breaking Bad* was “rigged” toward Walt's perspective, even in scenes without him. That is a useful warning. Alignment is not moral truth.

For each episode, decide:

- whose problem organizes the hour;
- what that person cannot perceive;
- whose pain the current framing minimizes;
- where the episode briefly breaks alignment and lets another reality in.

The Joined must never be reduced to pod-people because the unjoined fear them. The unjoined must never be reduced to irrational holdouts because the Joined pity them.

### 9. Mix tones through behavior, not relief valves

The comedy, dread, tenderness, and violence should arise from the same situation. Do not stop the drama for a joke or signal solemnity after a joke.

Useful tonal engines include:

- precise bureaucracy meeting an unprecedented event;
- competent people forced to use inadequate language;
- literal answers exposing euphemism;
- grand consequences emerging from shabby rooms and ordinary objects;
- a character protecting dignity at the worst possible time.

Nobody needs to be “the comic character.”

### 10. End with moral recognition, not just revelation

A twist changes what happened. A strong ending changes what the choice means.

The best final turns combine:

- **plot recognition:** what has actually happened;
- **character recognition:** what the person has been doing or becoming;
- **moral cost:** why success and failure are now inseparable;
- **visual afterimage:** one image that contains the contradiction without explaining it.

## 3. The room's working documents

Maintain four living ledgers. These matter more than a thick mythology bible.

### A. Choice-and-consequence ledger

| Choice | Stated reason | Private reason | Immediate success | Residue | Later consequence | Who pays? |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Synthesize S-78 | Study it before others do | Mara needs uncertainty to yield | A viable particle is produced | Protocol, samples, prestige, access | Human infection becomes possible | Jenn first; everyone later |

The “who pays?” column prevents elegant causality from becoming morally abstract.

### B. Knowledge ledger

For each major character and the audience, track:

- verified facts;
- strong suspicions;
- false beliefs;
- withheld information;
- the next fact capable of changing behavior.

Suspense usually depends less on hiding facts from the audience than on distributing them unevenly.

### C. Rules and logistics ledger

Track transmission dose, incubation, contagious period, testing limits, field effect, shielding, geographical spread, institutional authority, and elapsed time. Every exception requires either a rule revision or a visible price.

### D. Relationship debt ledger

For each central pair, record:

- what each wants from the other;
- the grievance neither will name;
- the last act of care;
- the last betrayal;
- the fact that would change the relationship;
- who currently has leverage.

This keeps procedural scenes emotionally loaded.

## 4. Season architecture

### Step 1: State the irreversible transformation

Write the starting and ending moral identities, not merely plot conditions.

- **World:** private, quarrelsome humanity → peaceful collective humanity.
- **Mara:** knowledge can make danger governable → nearly all knowledge cannot settle what ought to be governed.
- **Rios:** correct threat identification enables protection → being correct can make the threat deadlier.
- **Jenn:** invisible executor of others' work → origin point whose practical care remakes the species.

### Step 2: Choose six irreversible decisions

An episode earns its place by containing a choice that closes a door. Suggested series chain:

1. Mara argues successfully for synthesis.
2. Jenn, altered but still legible as Jenn, deliberately exposes another person.
3. Mara refuses orientation after someone she loves joins.
4. Rios classifies the Joined as a threat and wins authority to contain them.
5. The Joined choose acceleration after interpreting containment as inevitable violence.
6. Mara chooses an unproven intervention and loses the private self she meant to preserve.

The known canon outcome does not weaken suspense if the choices remain surprising, specific, and costly.

### Step 3: Build the chain backward and play it forward

Use the known endpoint only to identify prerequisites. Once episode work begins, ask what these people would actually do with the knowledge they possess now. Do not force them to preserve canon through implausible choices.

### Step 4: Escalate the definition of the problem

Each episode should replace the governing question:

1. **Can this signal be biological?**
2. **Has anything happened?**
3. **Is this illness, communication, or consent?**
4. **Can healthy people constitute an outbreak?**
5. **Which intervention causes less harm?**
6. **What remains of a person after humanity survives?**

Escalation is not simply larger scale. It is a harder moral category.

### Step 5: Preserve contingency

At least twice per episode, the audience should see a plausible path by which disaster could still be avoided. A character then closes that path for a reason we understand. Tragedy needs exits.

## 5. Episode-breaking template

Do not draft until the room can answer all twelve prompts.

1. **Episode owner:** Whose problem organizes the hour?
2. **Opening disequilibrium:** What has changed since the last final image?
3. **Cold-open promise:** What unforgettable process, question, or contradiction opens the hour?
4. **Concrete want:** What does the owner try to achieve today?
5. **Private need:** What emotional admission are they avoiding?
6. **Plan:** What competent method do they choose?
7. **First success:** How does the plan work?
8. **Turn:** What residue or opposing intelligence changes the problem?
9. **Choice under pressure:** What do they choose freely and why?
10. **Cost bearer:** Who other than the chooser absorbs the cost?
11. **Recognition:** What does the owner understand too late?
12. **Final image:** What physical image contains both victory and loss?

### Cold-open standard

A cold open must do at least three of these jobs:

- create a clean question;
- establish a process;
- introduce a consequential object;
- change scale or point of view;
- plant information that will mature later;
- establish the episode's tonal contradiction;
- become newly legible by the end of the hour.

“Mysterious” is not enough. A good teaser is precise before it is explained.

## 6. Scene-card template

Use one card per scene.

```text
SCENE:
POV / ALIGNMENT:
FIRST IMAGE:

WANT: What does the focal character need before leaving?
OBSTACLE: Who or what makes that difficult?
TACTIC: What do they physically or verbally do?
INFORMATION GAP: Who knows what?
TURN: What changes the tactic, leverage, or meaning?
CHOICE: What does someone decide?
RESIDUE: What survives the cut?

SUBTEXT: What cannot be said plainly?
VISUAL ACTION: Could the scene be followed with sound off?
LAST IMAGE:
NEXT-SCENE CAUSE: Why must the next scene happen now?
```

### Scene gate

Cut or combine a scene if it does not change at least two of the following:

- knowledge;
- leverage;
- plan;
- relationship;
- moral position;
- physical risk.

Atmosphere is valuable, but it should accumulate evidence or pressure while it breathes.

## 7. Suspense design

### The suspense equation

> **Legible goal + understood process + constraint + information imbalance + approaching point of no return**

The audience does not need constant surprise. It needs enough understanding to anticipate several bad possibilities.

### Preferred sources of tension

- A character performs a delicate process while hiding a different objective.
- Two competent people solve different versions of the same problem.
- The audience knows the danger but not which ordinary object carries it.
- A plan works while quietly transferring cost elsewhere.
- Someone can escape only by violating the value they claim to protect.
- The calmest person in the scene possesses the most destabilizing information.

### Avoid

- random equipment failure at the exact useful moment;
- a smart person refusing one obvious question;
- a late rule invented solely to save or doom someone;
- coincidence resolving a problem created by choice;
- anonymous mass harm used as emotional shorthand;
- mysteries prolonged after the dramatic question has become obvious.

## 8. Dialogue guide

### Default behavior

- Characters speak to accomplish something, not to explain the episode.
- Let professional vocabulary be exact, but make the conflict understandable through stakes and behavior.
- Prefer evasion, correction, bargaining, misdirection, and inconvenient honesty to speeches.
- Give characters different thresholds for uncertainty. Mara qualifies; Rios categorizes; Leah observes; Davis translates systems; Jenn solves the next practical problem.
- Let silence force the audience to read faces, objects, and space.

### The four-pass dialogue edit

1. **Information pass:** Remove facts both speakers already know.
2. **Intention pass:** Give every line a tactic.
3. **Character pass:** Could another character say this unchanged? If yes, rewrite or cut.
4. **Compression pass:** Enter after pleasantries; leave when the power changes.

### Joined dialogue

Define the truth rule precisely. “Cannot lie” is too broad unless the scripts know whether the Joined can:

- decline to answer;
- answer only the literal question;
- omit relevant facts;
- use uncertain language strategically;
- speak through one body while relying on another's knowledge;
- make a promise about future collective behavior.

Their dialogue becomes dramatically interesting when truthfulness does not equal full disclosure.

---

## 9. Visual and tonal guide

### Visual grammar

Before the Joining:

- imperfect repetitions;
- interrupted tasks;
- asymmetrical groups;
- private corners and obstructed sightlines;
- hands performing specialized work differently.

During spread:

- echoed gestures with slight delay;
- actions completed by a different body than the one that began them;
- eye-lines that imply knowledge crossing space;
- ordinary objects changing meaning through reuse.

After convergence:

- coordination that is purposeful, not ornamental;
- symmetry used sparingly enough to remain uncanny;
- isolated survivors breaking otherwise fluent patterns;
- no generic “hive mind” staging when a practical choice can show shared intelligence.

### Violence rule

Violence should be:

- infrequent;
- spatially understandable;
- difficult rather than cool;
- consequential after the cut;
- experienced by a specific person, not only by the plot.

When mass death arrives, stay with interruptible tasks, but also carry several named costs forward. Numbers establish scale; relationships establish loss.

### Montage rule

Every montage needs an internal story:

> baseline → variation → mastery or deterioration → exception → consequence

A collection of handsome process shots is not yet a dramatic montage.

## 10. Prequel-specific rules

### Do not dramatize trivia

A prequel should reveal why familiar facts hurt, not merely how they happened. Each canon connection must do at least one of these:

- reverse our judgment of a known event;
- charge an ordinary object or phrase with new emotion;
- expose the cost hidden by the parent story;
- create irony between what characters hope and what viewers know;
- deepen a theme the parent series cannot explore from its later vantage point.

If a beat exists only to make viewers recognize a name, number, suit, or line, cut it.

### Known outcome, unknown meaning

The audience may know the Joining occurs. Suspense therefore lives in:

- which reasonable decision makes it unavoidable;
- who could have stopped it and why they do not;
- what “peaceful for the first month” costs privately;
- whether the Joined are born from Jenn, from S-78, or from reciprocal human need;
- when a person ceases to be able to consent even while sounding lucid.

### Protect the parent series' negative space

Do not answer questions whose ambiguity generates *Pluribus*. Explain mechanisms only when a character needs the answer to choose. Preserve uncertainty about the sender's motive, immunity, reversibility, and the metaphysical status of the collective.

## 11. Diagnostic of the current treatment

### What is already at the quality bar

1. **The central contradiction is excellent.** “Each group acts out of care. Together, they end the world” is a strong engine, not just a theme.
2. **The Joined are sincere.** Refusing secret cruelty protects the difficult moral experiment.
3. **Process carries scale.** Pipettes, badges, coolers, valves, calls, and doughnuts give the premise a filmable physical life.
4. **Institutions behave legibly.** Astronomy, biology, public health, agriculture, and military systems each see a different valid problem.
5. **The speculative rule is disciplined.** One impossible field effect is surrounded by plausible biological constraints.
6. **The final handoff to Carol has emotional meaning.** Davis is not merely an Easter egg; he embodies the new world's sincere coercive care.

### Diagnostic status after the pre-outline reconciliation

The original diagnostic identified eight blockers. They are now resolved at treatment-and-ledger level:

1. **Viewpoint:** Mara is the declared continuous spine, with one governing owner per episode and no private viewpoint after convergence.
2. **Character causality:** Mara’s synthesis argument defeats observation-only caution; Rios applies a simultaneous-isolation doctrine she authored after an earlier failure; the Joined compare explicit harm models before choosing acceleration.
3. **Truth and consent:** fully Joined speakers cannot knowingly assert false facts, and peaceful deliberate exposure requires proactive material-risk disclosure and active assent.
4. **Transmission:** Arlo is infected when Jenn’s saliva reaches broken skin; brief touch remains ineffective.
5. **Antisense intervention:** the candidate is ordered in Part 2, tested and limited across Parts 3–4, concentrated in Part 5, and fails for an established mechanistic reason in Part 6.
6. **Evelyn’s final chain:** the equipment-fire-glass pileup is removed. Mateo dies despite Leah’s collective skill; Evelyn’s own Stage 4 transition then forces the autonomy conflict into her body.
7. **Mel’s exposure:** Jenn prepares a measured dose from the doughnuts Mel brought, discloses the route and risks, and recognizes that Mel’s assent cannot include Sofia.
8. **Named continuity:** Arlo, Mel, Sofia, Tess, Omar, Mateo, and Evelyn now have planted costs that recur rather than disappearing into montage.

The outlines must still prove these repairs scene by scene. In particular, every network fact needs a visible knowledge route, every accidental exposure must remain distinct from deliberate orientation, and the named-cost threads must appear before their payoff rather than being added retrospectively.

## 12. Recommended revision order

Do not polish dialogue yet.

1. Treat the viewpoint architecture, S-78 rules, truth, knowledge, consent, six-choice chain, and consequence ledgers as frozen unless scene breaking exposes a material contradiction.
2. Break all six episodes at scene level around their irreversible character decisions.
3. Trace every surprising network fact to a connected host, perception, memory, ordinary record, or ordinary device access.
4. Distinguish every accidental exposure from deliberate orientation and verify disclosure and assent before each peaceful deliberate exposure.
5. Seed Arlo, Mel, Sofia, Tess, Omar, Mateo, Evelyn, Tom, and the antisense intervention before their consequences mature.
6. Audit every canon connection for dramatic value beyond recognition.
7. Build cold opens and final images as paired visual arguments, then score every episode before prose drafting.
8. Only then write the calibration sample, sequences, and dialogue.

## 13. Quality-control scorecard

Score each episode from 0–2 on every question: **0 = absent, 1 = partial, 2 = undeniable**. An episode needs at least **20/24**, with no zero in causality, character choice, or rules.

| Test | Question |
|---|---|
| Premise | Does the hour test the central contradiction rather than merely mention it? |
| Ownership | Does one character's concrete problem organize the hour? |
| Causality | Does the decisive turn result from an established choice? |
| Character | Could only this person plausibly create this version of the problem? |
| Intelligence | Do capable people notice and respond to available evidence? |
| Rules | Do science, knowledge, truth, distance, and time remain consistent? |
| Process | Is at least one procedure made legible and dramatically variable? |
| Visuals | Do first images, physical actions, and last images carry meaning? |
| Suspense | Can viewers anticipate multiple outcomes without knowing which occurs? |
| Tone | Do humor, dread, and tenderness arise from the same reality? |
| Consequence | Does prior residue matter, and does new residue survive the hour? |
| Recognition | Does the ending change the moral meaning of what preceded it? |

### Final greenlight questions

Before a draft leaves the room, every writer should answer independently:

1. Where did I feel the writer forcing the outcome?
2. Where did a character become less intelligent for the plot?
3. Which scene could be removed without changing later behavior?
4. Which rule did I have to explain away?
5. What choice will still matter two episodes later?
6. What image do I remember without consulting the outline?
7. Whose suffering did the point of view hide?
8. Does the episode leave the moral question harder than it found it?

Any repeated answer is a revision priority.

## 14. A productive room protocol

Gilligan consistently credits collaborative rooms and story-breaking before pages. Adopt that discipline without turning it into ceremony.

### Phase 1: Possibilities

- No drafting.
- Generate competing choices, not just events.
- Preserve rejected ideas in a parking lot.
- Ask what each character believes at this exact moment.

### Phase 2: Prosecution

- Assign one writer to attack causality.
- Assign one to attack rules and logistics.
- Assign one to defend the opposing moral position.
- Assign one to track emotional and relationship residue.

Rotate these roles each episode.

### Phase 3: Silent test

Tell the episode using only first images, physical actions, turns, and final images. Any indispensable explanation reveals a sequence that needs a stronger visual carrier.

### Phase 4: Break

Board every beat. Connect cards explicitly with **because**, **but**, or **therefore**. Mark all coincidences and all new rules in red. Do not assign pages until the choice-and-consequence chain survives challenge.

### Phase 5: Draft and readback

After the first draft, update all four ledgers from what is actually on the page—not what the room intended. The produced story is the evidence.

## 15. Human-authorship standard

The artefacts produced from this guide—treatments, outlines, scene cards, character biographies, pitch documents, and scripts—must read as the work of particular writers making particular choices. Clean prose is welcome. Generic, frictionless prose is not.

The goal is not to “beat” an AI detector. Those tools are unreliable, and deliberately adding mistakes only makes the work worse. The goal is to remove habits that make prose feel generated: empty comprehensiveness, mechanical balance, repeated conclusions, second-hand emotion, and language with no authorial stake.

### A. Give every artefact a human situation

Before drafting, state:

- who is writing the artefact;
- who must read or use it;
- what decision it needs to enable;
- what the writer believes is most important;
- what can safely be omitted.

A room document should sound like something writers can argue with and alter. A pitch should sound like someone urgently showing another person why the series must exist. A treatment should create an experience, not inventory every virtue of the project.

### B. Prefer selection over completeness

Generated prose often tries to cover every aspect evenly. Human authors reveal judgment by choosing what deserves space.

- Develop the two strongest observations instead of listing seven adjacent ones.
- Allow minor characters, themes, and systems to receive unequal treatment when the story does.
- Do not add a “challenges,” “opportunities,” or “key takeaways” section by reflex.
- Do not restate the same idea as premise, principle, implication, and conclusion.
- End when the useful thought is complete.

An omission made knowingly is better than a paragraph included because the document seems expected to have one.

### C. Replace abstract praise with observed evidence

Avoid sentences such as:

- “This powerful moment deepens the emotional stakes.”
- “The sequence explores complex themes of identity and connection.”
- “This creates a compelling and thought-provoking contrast.”
- “Ultimately, the episode asks what it means to be human.”

These sentences report an intended effect without creating or proving it. Name the action, image, contradiction, or cost instead.

**Synthetic:** “The scene powerfully demonstrates Mara's increasing isolation.”

**Human:** “Mara gives the correct answer. Three people thank Deshpande.”

The second sentence supplies evidence and trusts the reader.

### D. Break mechanical symmetry

Use parallel construction only when the parallel itself carries meaning. Do not make every paragraph the same length, every character description follow the same sequence, or every episode summary land on an identical rhetorical flourish.

Watch for:

- repeated “not X, but Y” constructions;
- lists that always contain three polished abstractions;
- consecutive paragraphs beginning with a character's name;
- every section ending with a maxim;
- a colon or em dash in nearly every paragraph;
- repeated formulations such as “At its core,” “Crucially,” “Importantly,” “In this way,” and “Ultimately”;
- headings that merely rename the paragraph beneath them.

Rhythm should respond to content. A bureaucratic disaster may need exact, accumulating clauses. A death may need one plain sentence.

### E. Allow thought to have texture

Human prose can qualify, double back, become concrete, or admit an unresolved problem. It does not need to sound uncertain everywhere, but it should distinguish observation from inference.

- Name what the room has not solved.
- Preserve disagreements that matter.
- Say “we do not yet know” when that is the useful truth.
- Let one precise example carry a general point.
- Keep surprising, defensible details even when they disrupt a neat category.
- Do not manufacture confidence by converting every possibility into a rule.

Do not simulate humanity with typos, slang, fake personal memories, or random fragments. Specific judgment is the human signal.

### F. Protect character asymmetry in dialogue

No two characters should be equally eloquent, equally self-aware, or equally efficient at stating subtext.

During the dialogue pass, mark:

- the line that is too perceptive for its speaker;
- the line that exists only to help the audience;
- the line in which a character names their own emotional condition accurately;
- the joke that sounds written rather than used as a tactic;
- the speech whose interruptions and responses could carry half its meaning;
- any distinctive phrase repeated because the writer likes it, not because the character would.

Characters may search for a word, choose the wrong example, answer the safer question, change register around authority, or abandon a sentence when its intention has been achieved. Their language should be imperfect because their objectives conflict—not because the draft adds cosmetic mess.

### G. Keep production prose physical

Treatments and outlines should favor verbs, objects, locations, duration, and behavior over interpretive labels.

**Avoid:** “Tension rises as the team confronts the horrifying implications of their discovery.”

**Prefer:** “Nobody touches the sample. Deshpande pulls a waste bin under the table in case Jenn vomits again.”

After any paragraph containing two or more abstractions—words such as *humanity*, *identity*, *connection*, *morality*, *transformation*, or *stakes*—ask whether one can be replaced with something filmable.

### H. Remove prompt-shaped language

The final artefact must not mention the request, the assistant, the drafting process, or what the document “aims to” provide unless it is genuinely part of the production context.

Remove:

- “Here is a comprehensive…”
- “As requested…”
- “This guide will explore…”
- “It is worth noting that…”
- “There are several key reasons…”
- “In conclusion…” followed by a repeat of the opening;
- offers to generate additional sections;
- placeholders presented as decisions;
- claims that the material is “cinematic,” “compelling,” or “Gilligan-esque” without evidence.

The document should begin with the story, problem, or decision—not an account of having produced a document.

### I. Do not counterfeit authority

- Never invent a quotation, interview claim, scientific fact, production constraint, or canon detail.
- Attribute research when it changes a creative decision.
- Separate canon, inference, proposal, and unresolved question.
- Do not cite sources merely to decorate obvious claims.
- Do not imply that Gilligan or the *Pluribus* production team endorses this project or framework.

Nothing sounds more synthetic than false certainty wearing research as costume.

### J. The read-aloud and memory tests

Every outward-facing artefact receives two final passes.

**Read-aloud pass:** One writer reads the document without editing as they go. Another marks where attention drifts, where the cadence repeats, where a sentence sounds presentational, and where the writer naturally substitutes simpler words. Revise those places.

**Memory pass:** After a break, each reader writes down the three details they remember. If they remember only themes and claims rather than choices, images, or human behavior, the artefact is too abstract.

### Human-voice release checklist

Before delivery, confirm:

1. The opening contains a decision, image, claim, or problem—not throat-clearing.
2. No paragraph repeats the function of the paragraph before it.
3. General claims are supported by a concrete story fact.
4. Sentence and paragraph rhythms vary for a reason.
5. Lists are used only when the items genuinely need comparison.
6. The prose does not praise its own material.
7. Characters do not share one polished vocabulary.
8. Unresolved issues remain honestly unresolved.
9. Nothing claims more certainty than the evidence permits.
10. The final line advances or crystallizes the work instead of summarizing it again.
11. At least one other human has challenged a choice, not merely corrected grammar.
12. The named writer takes responsibility for the final selection and wording.

The last two checks matter most. A strong artefact is not human because it contains verbal imperfections. It is human because somebody exercised taste, rejected alternatives, accepted consequences, and stands behind the result.

## 16. Research basis

This framework is a synthesis, not a claim that Gilligan follows a published formula.

- Gilligan describes breaking episodes beat by beat, thinking visually, asking what appears first in each scene, and treating teasers as crucial hooks in [his *Time* interview](https://time.com/archive/7126406/vince-gilligan-the-skipper-a-breaking-bad-qa/).
- His [Television Academy oral history](https://interviews.televisionacademy.com/interviews/vince-gilligan) covers building stories “brick by brick,” the importance of visual elements, realism, tonal balance, character transformation, and how little of *Breaking Bad* was fixed in advance.
- In [*The New Yorker* interview](https://www.newyorker.com/culture/the-new-yorker-interview/vince-gilligan-wants-to-write-a-good-guy), he reflects on the moral distortion created by aligning *Breaking Bad* so strongly with Walt and on finding an ending whose consequence fits Jimmy's choices.
- The 2025 [*Time* profile on *Pluribus*](https://time.com/7328396/vince-gilligan-interview-pluribus/) identifies “actions have consequences” as a shared moral foundation of *Breaking Bad* and *Better Call Saul*, traces *Pluribus* to a benign wish-fulfilment premise turned unsettling, and documents Gilligan's commitment to collaborative authorship.
- The [Television Academy's *Better Call Saul* retrospective](https://www.televisionacademy.com/features/online-originals/better-call-saul-vince-gilligan) shows a development process willing to discard an initial format when the character and dramatic engine point elsewhere.
- [*Pluribus: The Official Podcast*](https://podcasts.apple.com/ca/podcast/pluribus-the-official-podcast/id1846198705) provides episode-level discussions with Gilligan, writers, performers, and department heads, reinforcing that the finished visual and tonal language is collaborative rather than the product of one writer alone.
- The linked [biographical overview](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vince_Gilligan) is useful as a map of Gilligan's credits and career, but the craft claims above rely mainly on direct interviews and institutional sources.

## The one-page room card

Pin this above the episode board:

> **What does the character want now?**  
> **What compromised need shapes the method?**  
> **What do we see them physically do?**  
> **Why is the choice defensible?**  
> **How does it work?**  
> **What residue survives success?**  
> **Who pays?**  
> **What becomes irreversible?**  
> **What does the final image make us reconsider?**

If the room can answer those questions with specificity, the work is moving toward Gilligan's quality standard while remaining unmistakably its own story.
