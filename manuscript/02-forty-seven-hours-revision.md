# FORTY-SEVEN HOURS

At 3:08 in the morning, Jenn crossed **MACAQUES** off the whiteboard.

Rabbits, mice and guinea pigs had already gone gray under months of dry-erase residue. Rats occupied the bottom line. Beside them, somebody from upstairs had written **FUNDING REVIEW — FRIDAY** and underlined it twice, as if the animals might find the second line persuasive.

Jenn rolled the marker between her palms.

The macaques had shown appetite, irritation, alliances and grudges. They had thrown food at Dr. Deshpande and behaved with exemplary restraint toward everyone else. They had not shown S-78 doing anything.

Neither had the rabbits, mice or guinea pigs.

The rats had until dawn.

She returned the marker to its clip and finished the overnight sheet. Weight stable. Feeding normal. Coat normal. No tremor, discharge, aggression or altered social behavior. The uneventful observations had become a kind of failure for which the technicians were expected to apologize.

The animal program occupied two floors and parts of three careers. Eight months earlier, senior officials had watched four pipettes descend toward four wells. Now the historic result lived in coded freezers whose storage fees appeared on Jenn's shift report.

An urgent message from project administration covered the bottom of her screen.

**FREEZER ALLOCATION RESPONSE REQUIRED.**

The attachment proposed moving one archive to a different secured unit before the funding review. Jenn checked the unit number. It was already full.

She replied with the capacity log, the maintenance hold and a photograph of the red tag on the door. The message returned three minutes later with a yellow exclamation point and no sign that anyone had opened the photograph.

Upstairs, the signal remained an extraterrestrial biological instruction set recovered from six hundred light-years away. Down here, it was why the freezer alarm and the vending machine could no longer share an outlet.

Jenn completed the rat-room inventory. One status light remained active after its cage card showed the animal due for euthanasia.

She checked the number twice.

* * *

The badge reader released her into the security vestibule at 3:17. Mel Ortega had installed two pink bakery boxes beside the sign-in terminal.

“No,” Jenn said.

“I haven't asked.”

“You're standing behind doughnuts at three in the morning. You don't have to.”

Mel opened the top box. Glaze shone under the fluorescent lights. A cartoon trombone in marching shoes had been drawn on the fundraiser label with purple marker.

“Sofia's band,” he said. “Uniforms.”

“What's wrong with their old uniforms?”

“They look like the school bought them.”

“The school did buy them.”

“Fourteen-year-olds can tell.”

Jenn checked the price, took out three dollars and selected a doughnut with a bald patch where the icing had adhered to the lid. The inner door chimed before she could bite it.

Mel glanced at the panel. “Rat room.”

She put the doughnut on the counter beside her name in the log. “Don't resell that.”

“I respect chain of custody.”

“You sell pastries beside a biometric lock.”

“That's how seriously I take it.”

The panel chimed again.

Jenn started to badge through, then noticed the security-review form under Mel's elbow. His name appeared at the top. The field marked **EMPLOYEE STATEMENT** remained blank.

“What happened?” she asked.

Mel shifted the doughnut box over the page. “Nothing.”

“Is nothing why you're fundraising at 3:17?”

“Sofia believes night workers are an underserved market.”

“Mel.”

He looked toward the empty corridor. “Door alarm last week. My badge was the last one through.”

“The latch fault?”

“Facilities says no fault found.”

“Then write that.”

“Security wants to know why I didn't observe the door resecure.”

“Did you?”

“I was breaking up a fight between two delivery drivers over whose pallet was more in the way.”

“Write that.”

“You make statements sound easy.”

Jenn pointed at the animal alarm. “Mine don't involve people.”

The sentence landed badly. They both knew her job involved people whenever the people upstairs needed an answer.

Mel placed his palm over the blank form. “Go. I'll accuse the pallet.”

Jenn badged through the red floor line.

Her doughnut remained under the trombone, slowly going stale.

* * *

Rat 4C lay on its side beneath the clear procedure cover.

It was due for euthanasia at the end of the shift. The cage card said male, adult, no clinical signs. Its black eye reflected the ceiling panel. Nothing moved except the cardiac trace.

Jenn leaned closer to the monitor.

“That lead's bad.”

Deshpande entered behind her, fastening the cuff of his gown. “Bad in what way?”

“It's reading a heartbeat.”

“That is usually the ambition.”

“Look at him.”

Deshpande looked from the animal to the trace. He checked the lead, then checked it again with the stubborn care of a man trying not to reward an impossible result with excitement.

The rat's heart was not merely beating. It was pounding.

Jenn read the cage number aloud. Deshpande matched it to the schedule. She checked the cover latch, the instrument tray and the red disposal container. The order mattered because order was what remained when the expected thing did not happen.

“Could be catatonic,” she said.

“Could be a word you selected because the correct one is unpleasant.”

“Dead?”

“That would be the unpleasant one.”

“Dead things are quieter in the chest.”

He reached for a penlight. Jenn moved to steady the cover.

The rat rose all at once.

Its teeth went through the glove at the side of her index finger. Pain arrived before fear had chosen a shape. Jenn pulled back. The rat struck the cover, righted itself and stopped again with the same terrible completeness.

Deshpande swore.

Jenn trapped the animal compartment before looking at her hand. The outer glove had two punctures. Blood filled one from beneath.

“Time,” Deshpande said.

The wall clock showed 3:36.

He reached for the emergency card. Jenn started the tap.

They performed the response in order. Secure the animal. Remove the outer glove without dragging the puncture across the skin. Wash. Encourage the wound to bleed. Call biosafety. Photograph the glove before sealing it. Speak the cage number, exposure route and time so the recorder did not have to infer any of them later.

“Bite,” Jenn said. “Right index finger. Through both layers.”

“Outer and inner glove breached,” Deshpande said.

“I just said both.”

“Both is a quantity. I am naming objects.”

“The recorder respects you more.”

The containment officer answered the wall phone and asked whether the animal remained secured.

Jenn looked at 4C. It had returned to the same unresponsive posture. The cardiac line continued to strike the screen.

“Secured,” she said.

“Human status?”

“Annoyed.”

Deshpande took the phone. “Conscious, oriented, active bleeding controlled.”

* * *

At 3:42, Jenn reached for a sterile pad and missed it.

Her hand had begun to shake. She held it up, irritated, as though catching it in the act might stop it. The tremor climbed her forearm, crossed her shoulders and tightened the muscles along her jaw. Her teeth struck once. Then again.

Deshpande pulled the waste bin away from the cabinet and guided her to the clear part of the floor.

“I'm not having a seizure,” she said.

“Good.”

“That wasn't reassurance.”

“It was data. Sit.”

Her knees made the decision first. She remained conscious while her body tried to obey instructions she had not given it. Her breath shortened. The room flashed sharp and white between blinks.

Deshpande crouched beyond the range of her legs. “Full name.”

She told him.

“Location.”

“Procedure room. At work. Still.”

“Do you know what happened?”

“Rat bit me.”

“Before that.”

“You doubted me.”

“Orientation intact.”

He kept her on the floor after the large movement stopped. Her fingers continued to twitch. The cardiac alarm from 4C sounded behind them with perfect indifference.

“Duration?” the containment officer asked over the phone.

Deshpande checked the clock. “Under two minutes. Residual fine tremor.”

“She needs medical observation.”

“I can hear you,” Jenn said.

“Good,” the officer replied. “That saves repetition.”

The event did not leave insight or peace behind. It left sweat under Jenn's collar, a bitten finger and the humiliation of six people arriving after the emergency was over.

The occupational-health physician photographed the wound again.

“We'll treat this as a potential exposure,” she said.

Jenn looked at the holes in her glove sealed inside an evidence bag.

“Which part is potential?”

“Exposure to S-78.”

“You mean infection.”

“I mean what I said.”

Jenn disliked her immediately. It was a relief to dislike someone for an ordinary reason.

They moved her into negative-pressure observation.

* * *

At hour two, the corridor lights dimmed and the observation window became a mirror.

The occupational-health physician wrote **POTENTIAL EXPOSURE** beneath the newest picture of Jenn's finger.

“I want access to my observation log,” Jenn said.

“You are the subject of the observation.”

“I'm the technician who performed the animal checks, restrained 4C and got bitten. If you want a reliable symptom history, I need to see what you've recorded.”

“You can provide corrections verbally.”

“To you.”

“Yes.”

“The person who keeps changing bite into potential exposure.”

The physician put down the stylus. Her badge said Dr. Lena Morris. Jenn had been reading the badge instead of her face because the badge was less likely to decide she was combative.

“The bite is documented,” Morris said. “The biological consequence is unknown.”

“Then write known bite, unknown consequence.”

“The category is potential exposure.”

“The category is wrong.”

Morris looked through the observation glass. Mel had arrived with a security officer and was pretending not to watch.

“If you leave this room,” Morris said, “security will stop you.”

“I'm not asking to leave.”

“You have asked three times.”

“I asked what authority you're holding me under. That's a different question.”

Morris named the occupational-health policy, the emergency containment authorization and the threshold at which the hospital would require a formal medical hold. Jenn made her repeat the last one so the recorder captured it.

They settled on a tablet that displayed Jenn's time-stamped observations but could not open the rest of the project record. In return, she agreed to stay without requiring security to convert an unlocked medical door into a detention measure nobody wanted to name.

Morris amended the note to **KNOWN BITE; POTENTIAL S-78 EXPOSURE**.

It was not Jenn's wording. It was closer.

* * *

At hour four, Arlo Baines arrived during the cleanup with a paper cut across his left knuckle and a new bandage in his pocket.

The cut came from a cardboard carton in Receiving, not from the lab. He showed it at the threshold. A safety officer inspected the break, told him to replace the loose strip and approved him for the room because no one had proposed that Jenn could transmit anything. The incident inside remained an animal bite. There was one exposed person, and that person was upstairs arguing about nouns.

Arlo pulled on fresh gloves.

He worked facilities nights because empty rooms made reasonable demands. A room could be released or not released. A disposal bin could be sealed or not sealed. Nobody asked a floor whether it was committed to the project.

The procedure room had been frozen at the moment Jenn left it. Her discarded gown lay inside the marked boundary. A damp fold caught against the disposal sleeve when Arlo lifted it. He stopped, reset his grip and felt a sting under the glove.

The safety officer made him hold still.

The new glove had split over the same knuckle. Contaminated material from Jenn's gown had pressed against the cut.

Arlo showed them. The skin was already red from the cardboard and bandage adhesive. The officer photographed it, cleaned it and opened the facilities form.

**BIOLOGICAL EXPOSURE** was not one of the available boxes. The choices were chemical, sharps, ergonomic, slip/fall and housekeeping.

The officer selected housekeeping and typed a note.

Arlo read it upside down. “You wrote minor.”

“Do you need a doctor?”

“No.”

“Then minor is accurate.”

“That's not the same thing.”

The officer added **EMPLOYEE DECLINED MEDICAL EVALUATION**.

“I declined a doctor,” Arlo said. “I didn't decline you writing what happened.”

The officer turned the screen so he could read the complete note. It described the split glove and existing cut. It did not connect either to Jenn because her record lived in another system and the cleanup instructions treated her material as a containment concern, not a transmissible one.

Arlo watched that become the official version of what he had said.

His form entered the facilities system. Jenn's entered the biological incident system. The two records traveled away from each other before anyone compared the times.

* * *

For twelve hours, every test was normal.

Mara arrived at hour six in the same blue suit she had worn to the first authorization hearing. Under the powered respirator she looked less like a scientist than a beekeeper on her way to court.

She brought a sampling schedule, a cognitive baseline and digital copies of the protocols Mara had once carried into the authorization fight. The thick construction protocol had produced eight months of nothing. The thin observation protocol now had a human subject.

“How do you feel?” Mara asked through the intercom.

“Observed.”

“Headache?”

“No.”

“Pressure behind the eyes?”

“No.”

“Thirst?”

Jenn looked at the empty water pitcher. “The air is dry.”

Mara recorded that without agreeing.

The baseline procedure had been written for a possible animal exposure, then revised after Jenn's acute event. Every four hours, Morris checked orientation, temperature, pupils, coordination and the bite. Every six, the adjacent laboratory processed paired samples under coded identifiers. Jenn completed short memory, language and visual-matching tasks designed to reveal change without exhausting her into one.

The purpose was simple enough to state: establish what remained stable before deciding what counted as deviation.

The first constraint was harder. Nobody knew which deviation mattered.

At hour seven, Jenn matched shapes and remembered word lists. At hour eight, she walked heel to toe along tape placed on the floor. At hour ten, Deshpande changed the word order to prevent practice from masquerading as improvement.

“You moved apricot,” Jenn said.

“You noticed.”

“That was the task.”

“The task was recall.”

“Then stop manipulating fruit.”

At hour eleven, the bite was swollen within the range Morris expected from an ordinary rat bite. Jenn's temperature remained normal. The cardiac trace from Rat 4C had settled after the animal was secured in a separate enclosure. It remained alive and unresponsive.

Deshpande argued for moving Jenn to ordinary occupational observation after the second normal panel. The intercom stayed open.

“She is clinically stable,” he said.

“After an unexplained autonomic event,” Mara said.

“That resolved.”

“It occurred.”

“Both can be true.”

Jenn looked from one to the other through the glass. Deshpande's proposal would give her a room with a window, ordinary medical staff and a door that was less philosophical about locking. Mara's caution would keep her beside the only laboratory equipped to notice something new.

Neither choice was stupid. That made listening worse.

Morris asked Jenn whether she wanted the transfer.

For the first time since the bite, somebody had asked what she wanted without placing the answer inside a test.

Jenn looked at the normal grid on Mara's screen. If the independent set returned normal, the whole event could close as a rat bite followed by stress. She could leave before the funding review and watch somebody upstairs convert **NO CLINICAL SIGNS** into a result with their name on it.

“I want access to my data if I move,” she said.

Mara answered before Morris. “You have access here.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Deshpande said, “The access follows the subject.”

Mara turned toward him. He had made the promise on behalf of a project he did not administratively control.

“Then document it,” Jenn said.

He did.

At hour twelve, the independent panel remained normal.

Mara asked for one final set before transfer. Deshpande called that delay defensible in the tone he used when he wanted Mara to hear the word *delay*.

Jenn remained in the negative-pressure room.

At hour fourteen, the funding review proceeded without her.

An automatic notice appeared on her tablet because she remained the assigned technician on the rat protocol. The attached summary described eight months of animal work as **NO OBSERVED PHENOTYPE** and recommended reducing the overnight program while the archive was consolidated.

Jenn read the sentence twice.

Rat 4C remained alive in isolation with an unexplained cardiac trace and an event severe enough to put her behind glass. The funding slide had been prepared the day before. Nobody had changed it because the review team considered the incident unconfirmed.

She called Deshpande through the intercom.

“Your slide is wrong.”

He was in the review room. Faces turned toward the speaker behind him.

“Which slide?” he asked.

“No observed phenotype.”

“The animal event is under investigation.”

“Then you observed an event.”

The program administrator leaned toward Deshpande's microphone. “We cannot classify one ambiguous presentation as a phenotype.”

“I didn't ask you to classify it. I asked you not to say you didn't observe it.”

“The phrase refers to reproducible study endpoints.”

“Then write that.”

Mara appeared at the edge of the review screen. “Jenn is right.”

The administrator looked irritated by the source, not the correction.

Mara continued. “Replace it with no confirmed S-78-associated phenotype. Add the 4C event and human incident as unresolved deviations. Defer archive consolidation until both are reviewed.”

The slide changed while Jenn watched. Her bite became a bullet point. Her name did not.

Deshpande said, “The observation came from Jenn.”

The administrator added **TECHNICIAN OBSERVATION: J. — CONFIRMATION PENDING**.

It was a small piece of authorship, badly formatted and secured behind classification. Jenn saved the revised slide to her permitted record anyway.

The funding review ended without reducing the program. Her work remained alive because she had been injured by it.

The observation window darkened into a mirror again. Her own face had become the longest-running abnormal result.

* * *

At hour nineteen, coffee arrived.

Jenn lifted the lid and waited for the smell.

Heat touched her face. Nothing else did.

She checked the label, then the date on the milk, then the coffee itself with the suspicion of a person examining a counterfeit bill.

The nurse brought peppermint, alcohol and the orange peel from her lunch. Jenn failed all three and designed six more tests to demonstrate that the first three had been unfair.

She failed those too.

Morris stood outside the glass with the odor cards.

“Any nasal congestion?”

“No.”

“Altered taste?”

Jenn drank the coffee. It was bitter and warm. “Taste is present.”

“Metallic taste?”

“No.”

“Salivation?”

“You can just ask whether my mouth is wet.”

“Is it?”

“Too wet for this coffee.”

Morris entered the responses. Jenn entered her own. The two records agreed except for the phrase **SUBJECT REPORTS**, which Morris's system inserted before every fact Jenn supplied.

At hour twenty-one, she shivered once hard enough to spill water onto the blanket. Her temperature stayed normal. At hour twenty-three, she requested another pitcher and began rationing it to avoid giving Mara the satisfaction of a visible pattern.

She did not hide the requests. She recorded them herself.

That distinction mattered until she tried to explain why.

At hour twenty-six, Deshpande entered holding her incident file open to a facilities note.

He placed the morning word cards on the table, then set the file beside them.

“We're changing the order again,” he said.

“Apricot is innocent.”

“Apricot has been removed.”

“You always punish the memorable one.”

He showed her the first card. Jenn named it. He showed the second. She named it and supplied its partner from the earlier set. On the fifth, he stopped the test to make a note in the incident file.

“During cleanup,” he began, “which hand did—”

“Left.”

He stopped.

Jenn looked at the page. She could not see the line from the bed.

“Which hand did who what?” she asked.

“Arlo Baines. He reported a cut.”

“Left knuckle.”

“Were you present?”

“You know I wasn't.”

“Did someone tell you?”

“No.”

The answer had arrived with the physical certainty of knowing where her own left hand was under the blanket. She tried to inspect the thought and found no memory attached to it.

Deshpande turned the file so she could see the note. **LEFT KNUCKLE** appeared halfway down.

“You read it upside down,” Jenn said.

“From there?”

“Maybe before you came in.”

“The file wasn't in the room.”

“Then you cued me.”

“How?”

“You looked left.”

He had not. They both knew it.

Deshpande moved the word cards out of reach. “What did it feel like?”

“A guess.”

“Before or after I started the question?”

“During.”

“Did you hear words?”

“Yours.”

“Other words.”

“No.”

“See the page?”

“No.”

“See Arlo?”

“I wouldn't know him if I did.”

Deshpande closed the file because one of them had to stop the test.

“You're going to report it,” Jenn said.

“It occurred during observation.”

“That is not an answer.”

“Yes.”

He did not tell her it was probably nothing. His refusal to comfort her felt like respect until Mara arrived and thanked him for the finding.

Mara stood outside the glass reading his note. “Good catch.”

She meant Deshpande.

Jenn turned the coffee cup until its label faced the wall.

* * *

At hour thirty-one, she slept under fluorescent light.

Not about the rat. From it.

Warm bodies pressed nearby. Food existed as smell and position. Gloved hands entered from above. There was no cage because a cage required an outside, and the dream contained no place the body was not.

Every so often, something failed to happen.

The absence returned with patience. Not sound. Not light. A missing sensation in a place Jenn did not possess while awake.

She woke with her bitten finger against her lips.

On the tablet she wrote: **Light from low angle. No edge to body. Repeating absence. Not my memory.**

She nearly deleted the last sentence.

Instead she added the time.

Deshpande read the note from the corridor. “Rat 4C?”

“I don't know.”

“You described the enclosure.”

“I described a dream.”

“You labeled it not my memory.”

“I was asleep. My standards were lower.”

“Your standards were specific.”

Jenn looked at the wall behind him. “You gave Mara credit for my answer.”

“I gave Mara my observation.”

“Of me.”

“Yes.”

“Which is different from my observation how?”

Deshpande removed his glasses. The marks on either side of his nose remained after them.

“It is different because I can document what I saw and you can document what you experienced. If we make those the same record, one of us disappears.”

“Guess which one.”

He put the glasses back on. “Then don't let me.”

The answer made her angrier because it required work from her.

* * *

At hour thirty-four, Mara held two result sheets against the glass.

“There is a low trace in the local sample,” she said. “Nothing in blood.”

“Contamination,” Deshpande said.

“Possibly.”

“From the animal.”

“Possibly.”

“You enjoy that word too much.”

“It is doing honest work.”

Mara walked Jenn through what the sheets could support. Something matching S-78 had appeared near the bite. It had not appeared in circulating blood. The local result might represent animal material carried into the wound, an invalid signal, or early activity confined to the exposure site. Independent confirmation was already underway.

“Does this explain the smell loss?” Jenn asked.

“No.”

“The thirst?”

“No.”

“The left hand?”

Mara paused. “No.”

“Good meeting.”

“It changes what we do next.”

Mara opened a separate antisense-candidate line in case the trace represented new S-78-complex manufacture. She did not call it treatment. She did not tell Jenn it would work. No target or preparation detail entered Jenn's record. The work order contained only its purpose and the fact that time might matter.

“For me?” Jenn asked.

“For evidence first.”

“That's an answer.”

“It is the only answer I have.”

“You have a lot of those.”

Mara's expression hardened, then changed. “I authorized the work that put that animal in front of you.”

“I performed the work that put the animal in front of me.”

“Both facts can exist.”

Deshpande looked at Mara. She heard her own phrase too late.

Jenn wanted the apology and resented the version that made Mara the principal actor even in regret.

“Order the confirmation,” she said. “Put my observation in the same packet.”

Mara did.

Their competence kept Jenn in the room. Their competence also kept everyone close enough to watch the next thing happen.

* * *

At hour thirty-eight, Jenn began the third sleep interval and failed to sleep.

The room had acquired private weather. Cool air descended from the vent. The bed trapped heat under her shoulders. Water tasted cold and faintly sweet. The coffee on the table remained odorless enough to be an accusation.

She tried to list the symptoms without arranging them into a story.

Bite. Acute tremor. Thirst. Smell loss. One shiver. A correct answer without a source. A dream from an angle no human would choose.

Each fact could be small alone. Mara's job was to determine whether together they deserved a larger noun. Jenn's job, apparently, was to continue producing them.

At hour forty, panic entered below her feet.

She was sitting on the bed with a normal pulse. The panic arrived anyway: a clenched chest, the expectation of bad news, the shame of being observed having both.

Jenn pressed two fingers against her wrist.

Steady.

She checked the monitor in case her fingers had become optimistic. Sixty-eight. No alarm.

The feeling had direction. That was the part she could not forgive.

First it pointed toward the security desk.

Mel stood there on the corridor camera, smiling at a delivery driver with his shoulders held too high. His review statement lay facedown beside the doughnut boxes. Jenn watched him touch his breastbone when the driver turned away.

The panic made sense once she saw the body carrying it. That did not mean it had come from him. She might have noticed his posture before the feeling and reversed the order in memory.

Jenn looked away from the camera.

The panic remained.

Then another one opened downward and left.

Supply room. Yellow chair. A hand held above a wastebasket because looking at the red knuckle had become worse than looking at anything else.

Arlo.

Jenn had met him twice. Once when he repaired a wheel on a rack, once when a supervisor thanked Deshpande for a room Arlo had cleaned. She did not know the color of any chair on the facilities floor.

She stood and walked to the observation-room wall. The nurse told her to return to bed.

“I'm testing position.”

“For what?”

“I don't know yet.”

The feeling sharpened near the wall and blurred when she stepped back. Jenn repeated the movement twice. On the third attempt nothing changed.

She wrote **POSSIBLE SPATIAL ASSOCIATION — NOT REPEATABLE** on her tablet and disliked how quickly professional language made fear presentable.

Then she deleted *spatial*. She had no evidence of location except the location itself.

Jenn pressed the nurse-call button.

“Can you contact facilities?”

“For what?”

“Ask whether Arlo Baines reported to occupational health.”

The nurse checked. Her expression changed before she spoke.

“He just did.”

“Where?”

“Two floors down.”

Jenn looked at the blank wall. The chair remained yellow behind it. She knew this as firmly as she knew the wall was white.

“Put that in my log,” she said.

“Which part?”

Jenn looked at the sentence she had begun and erased it.

“That I asked for him before you confirmed he was there.”

The smaller truth entered the record.

* * *

Mara arrived with Arlo's facilities record on one half of her screen and Jenn's biological record on the other.

The systems had assigned them different categories. The timestamps put his injury four hours after her bite.

Mara read the cleanup note, then asked project security to retrieve the room video without altering either incident record. The video showed Arlo's preexisting bandage at the threshold, the split glove and the stopped cleanup. It did not show what had moved from Jenn's gown to the cut clearly enough to answer whether exposure occurred.

“It was treated as contaminated material,” Jenn said.

“Material from the room,” Mara said.

“From my gown.”

“The video does not establish that.”

“The gown wore itself?”

“I am not arguing with you. I am naming what we can prove.”

Deshpande stood behind Mara. “The gown was hers.”

Mara changed the note from **UNRESOLVED FACILITIES EVENT** to **POSSIBLE SECONDARY EXPOSURE**.

“Did you see Arlo during cleanup?” she asked Jenn.

“I was here.”

“Did he enter before you were removed?”

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you about the cut before hour twenty-six?”

“No.”

“You asked for Arlo at hour forty before anyone on this floor knew he had reported.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Jenn felt the orderly machinery of the investigation closing around the fact. It was what the machinery had been built to do.

“I had a feeling.”

Mara waited.

“Panic.”

“Yours?”

Jenn looked at her own trace. Seventy beats per minute now. “Apparently not.”

“Did you perceive anything else?”

The yellow chair pressed against the thought before Jenn answered.

“Nothing I can verify.”

It was true and arranged to become less true.

Mara called for Arlo to receive medical assessment and opened the paired-observation contingency Deshpande had written after the left-hand answer.

The procedure was designed to protect evidence from the subjects. If a second exposed person showed a correlated event, the two would move to separate facilities before conscious or unconscious cueing could train the effect. Each site would use different staff, different task order and independent clocks. Contact between subjects would stop.

“You're treating communication as contamination,” Jenn said.

“We don't know that it is communication,” Mara said.

“You don't know that it's contamination.”

“Correct. The separation preserves both hypotheses.”

“By preventing the event.”

Deshpande said, “If it requires coaching, that is part of the event.”

Jenn looked at him. He had defended her observation record. Now he was explaining how to take away the only person who might share it.

“Would you separate two instruments?” she asked.

“If one instrument could teach the other what to report.”

“Good thing I'm a person.”

“That is why there are medical teams on both transfer orders.”

He meant it as care. Jenn heard the efficiency.

* * *

At hour forty-one, Arlo appeared on a video call from occupational health.

The camera showed him in a blue chair, not the yellow one. His left hand was wrapped. He looked tired and offended by the attention.

Morris conducted the assessment from Jenn's floor while a second clinician sat with him. They established his baseline from employment records and direct questions. No fever. Normal coordination. Inflamed cut. Poor sleep.

“Any unusual thirst?” Morris asked.

Arlo thought. “No.”

“Yes,” Jenn said.

Everyone on the call looked at her.

Arlo's mouth had gone dry. She knew this without seeing him swallow. The knowledge occupied her own tongue.

“I didn't report thirst,” he said.

“Are you thirsty?”

He looked toward the clinician beside him, then at a water cup on the table.

“Now that you said it.”

Morris made a note.

“That's cueing,” Mara said.

“I know,” Jenn said.

She had supplied them the explanation before they could use it against her. The fact remained.

They tested smell with ordinary controls. Arlo passed. They tested orientation and recall. He performed within baseline. Jenn watched his hands. He kept the left one elevated and rubbed the dry fingertips of his right against the chair arm.

Yellow vinyl flashed beneath them.

Jenn gripped her own bed rail until the image vanished.

At hour forty-three, Arlo's heart rate rose without a question preceding it. Jenn's followed several seconds later. Both returned to baseline when the clinicians stopped the interview.

Mara marked the result as temporally adjacent, not correlated. Separate clocks had not yet been synchronized closely enough to support the stronger word.

Jenn respected the distinction. She hated that too.

At hour forty-four, Deshpande brought her a printed copy of the transfer procedure.

“North facility for me,” she said. “South for Arlo.”

“Subject identifiers were randomized before assignment.”

“That makes the distance impersonal.”

“It makes it less likely anyone selected the easier trip for you.”

She read the medical provisions. The receiving teams were competent. Contact could resume after independent observation if no correlation was confirmed. The procedure contained no punishment and no careless gap she could attack.

“When?”

“If the correlation threshold is met, immediately. If not, reassess in the morning.”

“Who decides threshold?”

“Mara, Morris and me. Any two.”

“Not me.”

“You are one of the measurements.”

“I'm also the only person who knows what the measurement feels like.”

Deshpande pulled a chair to the glass but did not sit. “Then give us language we can use.”

Jenn almost told him about the yellow chair.

“It isn't language,” she said.

“Try badly.”

“You first.”

He waited.

“What do you want this to be?” she asked.

“Observable.”

“That's not what.”

“It is the only answer I trust myself to give.”

He left the procedure with her.

At hour forty-six, the transfer forms waited for signatures.

Mara stood between them at the workstation. The north team was awake. The south team had confirmed transport. One witnessed correlation would start both movements.

On the video call, Arlo sat alone for the first time while his clinician conferred outside the room. He examined the red skin above his bandage and tried not to look frightened by it.

Jenn felt his effort before she saw his face.

Mara asked, “Has anything else changed?”

There was a clean exit in front of her. Report the yellow chair, the thirst and the emotion arriving from another floor. Transfer them. Preserve independent evidence before their attention changed whatever was forming between them.

Jenn looked at the two destinations.

“No,” she said.

The word produced pressure behind her eyes.

Mara did not sign. Not yet.

* * *

At hour forty-seven, Arlo recognized Jenn.

It was not her name. It was the sensation of seeing a face twice: first as features, then as the person who belonged to them. The recognition reached her through the wall a fraction before Arlo's eyes focused on her image.

He gripped the edge of the blue chair.

“What was that?” he said.

The clinician reentered and moved toward him. His heart-rate alarm began to climb.

Jenn touched the cold rail of her bed and felt, impossibly, cracked vinyl beneath Arlo's right thumb.

“Arlo. Listen to me.”

Mara reached for the transfer form.

“Don't chase it,” Jenn said. “Pick one thing that's already there.”

“Where?”

“Under your hand. The split in the chair.”

Arlo lifted his hand. The camera could not see beneath it. He looked anyway.

His breathing stopped for one beat.

“How do you know that?”

“I don't. That's why you have to look.”

He bent far enough to see under the chair arm. Jenn received a brief, ridiculous impression of clear tape holding the vinyl together. She closed her eyes.

“Tape,” she said.

Arlo looked into the camera.

Recognition came again. This time he allowed it to remain.

Jenn felt the observation room from where he sat: white wall, glass, a cup of coffee she could not smell. She withdrew—not by pushing, which made the sensation worse, but by attending to the bed rail under her own hand until it became only cold metal.

“Now let go,” she said.

Arlo stared at his wrapped knuckle. His pulse began to slow.

Jenn counted her breaths. Four in. Six out. The rhythm was arbitrary; that was useful. It belonged to something she had chosen.

Two floors below, Arlo followed.

Mara stood between the two unsigned transfer forms.

“Jenn,” she said. “What changed?”

There was still an exit. Jenn could give her the chair, the tape, the recognition that had arrived before Arlo's face. The machinery would close the distance by making the distance enormous. It would call that protection, and on every form it would be correct.

Arlo waited below her. Not for an instruction. For proof that he had not invented her.

Jenn looked at Mara.

“Nothing I can separate from stress yet.”

The pressure behind her eyes sharpened and passed.

Mara watched her for a long moment. Then she set the transfer forms facedown.

Jenn told herself she had preserved the observation until morning.

In the darkened room, her monitor drew a quick rhythm and began to settle.

Two floors below, Arlo sat upright in the blue chair. His monitor followed hers by a fraction of a second, corrected past it, and returned.

Neither of them slept.
