# BEFORE WE WERE US

## Part One: Four Notes

Four toner cartridges stood open on the floor while Luis Ruiz proved the printer innocent.

It took eleven minutes. The astronomers continued behaving as though he had failed to locate the crime.

Paper covered the operations room in layers. Some sheets held the usual gray waterfalls of radio noise. Others carried vertical marks in red, green, blue and black, added by hand after somebody decided the printer could no longer be trusted with color. The marks drifted right, stopped, and started again. Four uncapped pens rolled along the table whenever anyone leaned against it.

Luis removed a strip of crumpled paper from the feed path and held it up.

“This is the jam.”

Nobody looked.

“The jam is why it stopped printing. It is not why it printed what you sent it.”

Bob Kaplan raised his head from three monitors. He had the wet, furious eyes of a man who had recently discovered blinking was optional.

“Run the test page,” he said.

Luis already had. He placed it on top of the nearest stack: four perfect bars, each ending where the manufacturer intended. Cyan, magenta, yellow, black. No banding, no offset, no evidence that the machine had invented a message from the sky.

“Again,” Bob said.

“It will be the same printer.”

“Please.”

Luis ran it again because equipment repair was mostly the art of proving the same thing until the owner could bear it. The page emerged warm and flawless.

Outside the windows, dawn flattened the radio dishes into pale shapes waiting for the rest of the landscape. One moved, then another. Their motors could not be heard through the glass.

An astronomer named Priya drew four new marks on a fresh sheet. She checked the clock taped above her terminal.

“Seventy-eight,” she said.

Bob moved to her screen. The room tightened around whatever he saw there.

Luis clipped the access panel shut. “Seventy-eight what?”

“Seconds.”

“Since?”

“The last one.”

On Priya's monitor, four narrow pulse positions rose out of the noise. They vanished. A timer in the corner began counting again.

Luis waited through the interval because everyone else did.

At seventy-eight seconds, the four positions returned carrying a different arrangement. Priya marked them in color. Bob pulled the page toward himself and laid it beside the previous page. The sequences differed. Their spacing did not.

“So it repeats,” Luis said.

“The carrier repeats,” Priya said. “The content changes.”

“Then why print all of it?”

Neither astronomer answered. Bob was sorting pages across the floor, aligning the hand-drawn marks into longer columns.

Luis opened the electronic work order. The available causes were mechanical wear, electrical fault, driver error, consumable failure and user damage. He selected **NO FAULT FOUND**.

Behind him, Priya stopped marking the next recurrence.

“We've got four states,” she said.

Bob stood. “Call the university.”

“Which department?”

He looked down at the coloured pages as though one of them might have printed the answer.

“Find me somebody who knows what four can mean.”

The repaired printer idled beside them. Nobody noticed.

* * *

Mara Velez muted the graduate student before the student had technically earned it.

“The model demonstrates a statistically meaningful—”

His mouth continued moving on the left side of her screen. On the right, a secure window displayed four white traces against black and a timer counting down from seventy-eight.

Mara slid her office door shut with one foot. The student had arranged his results into four colors because the department template offered four colors. She knew this. The coincidence still irritated her.

An observatory administrator filled the top square of the secure call. Two astronomers occupied the square beneath him, cropped at the shoulders by a camera pointed too low.

“Is that the complete capture?” Mara asked.

“It's the complete transcription,” Bob said.

“I asked about the capture.”

“The raw stream is large.”

“So is the universe.”

The administrator cleared his throat. “Doctor Velez, we were advised that you consult across information theory and molecular systems.”

“Who advised you?”

“The Department of Energy.”

That did not answer her question. It did answer several others.

She unmuted her student long enough to say, “Send me the residuals and remove the phrase statistically meaningful from every slide where you have not stated meaningful to whom.”

“Now?” he asked.

“Before your committee discovers adjectives.”

She ended the university call.

On the secure window, the pulse arrangement returned. Mara ignored the astronomers' coloured transcription and requested a short segment of raw data, plus independent captures and the clock source for each instrument.

Bob's expression suggested she had asked for the dish.

“We have already excluded local interference,” he said.

“Then it should survive being excluded again.”

The files arrived one at a time. Mara checked the recurrence against the observatories' separate clocks. The interval held. She checked whether the four positions might be one event arriving through multipath distortion. They changed independently. She asked whether orientation, weather or pointing correction altered the pattern. Nothing in the logs moved with it.

The administrator tried to bring the conversation back to language.

“The working hypothesis is a four-symbol code.”

“A code is what we call a pattern after deciding someone meant us to read it.”

“Do you have a better noun?”

“Not yet.”

She divided the stream at the recurring interval, then at the smaller internal spacings. The four states did not behave like letters in ordinary text. Their long-range repetition was too structured in some places and too uneven in others. Blocks returned with small variations, nested inside larger blocks. There were stretches that looked less like statements than instructions with conserved parts.

Mara enlarged one region. She felt the idea before she trusted it, which was the wrong order and therefore worth distrusting.

Four states. Repeated architecture. Variation without the statistical habits of language.

The timer reached zero. Another arrangement appeared.

“Has anyone tried a biochemical alphabet?” she asked.

Bob leaned closer to his camera. “Meaning?”

Mara assigned the four observed states abstract labels. She did not decide which state corresponded to which base; the mapping could wait. What mattered was whether any assignment produced the layered redundancies and instruction-like regions expected of biological information.

One did.

Not cleanly. Not in a family she recognized. But the structure stopped looking arbitrary.

Mara did not trust the relief that followed. She asked Priya to prepare three comparison sets: one from a different interval of the capture, one with the state labels reassigned, and one whose internal order had been scrambled. Priya was not to tell her which was which.

“You think you're seeing what you want?” Bob asked.

“I think wanting is cheap.”

The sets arrived without labels. Mara ran the same coarse comparisons over each. One retained its nested repetitions across distant portions of the capture. One produced short accidental patterns that disappeared when the window moved. One was uniformly graceless.

She selected the first.

Priya revealed it as the untouched interval under the biochemical mapping.

That still did not prove the mapping. It proved only that the structure survived a small attempt to embarrass it.

Mara asked for an interval from the second observatory, one nobody in Bob's room had transcribed. The same conserved regions returned around different internal content. Independent instrument, independent clock, no shared printer and no opportunity for the coloured pens to create agreement.

On her desk, the muted graduate student sent a message asking whether he should reschedule.

Mara typed **YES** and did not send it. Reschedule implied she knew what would happen next.

The administrator said, “Doctor?”

Mara should have asked for an independent team. She should have written the result down before giving the room a word it could never return.

“Nucleotide,” she said.

Every face in the call grid went still.

“You're saying it's DNA?” Priya asked.

“No.”

“RNA?”

“I am saying the four states behave more usefully as a nucleotide alphabet than as language. That is not the same claim.”

The administrator reached beyond his camera. The public-network cable came free from the wall. One of the video squares vanished, then returned through a slower secure relay.

Mara watched the student presentation disappear beneath a classification banner.

By the time she was permitted to call her department chair, somebody else had already canceled her afternoon.

* * *

The observatory gave visitors padded envelopes for their phones. The envelopes had been purchased for failed disk drives and were just wide enough to make every device look damaged.

Mara wrote her name across the seal. An intelligence liaison named Grace Wu watched the ink dry before placing the envelope in a numbered tray.

“Laptop?” Grace asked.

“Office.”

“Watch?”

Mara held up her bare wrist.

“Other storage media?”

“Working memory.”

Grace did not smile. “That leaves with you.”

“Then your procedure has a gap.”

“The procedure has an assumption about your patriotism.”

“Less comforting.”

Mara had been driven from the university without being told whether she was under escort. The same unmarked car would return her if she finished before it acquired a second purpose.

Inside the conference room, the paper stacks from dawn had been converted into an evidence display. Each page carried a time, instrument identifier and initials. Luis's work order sat at the corner beneath a clear sleeve: **NO FAULT FOUND**.

Grace opened a government packet. The first page described the signal as an extraterrestrial four-state transmission of unknown intent. The second page contained the working transcription.

Mara read five lines and stopped.

“This block is wrong.”

Bob folded his arms. “It was checked twice.”

“Against what?”

“The printed record.”

“The printed record breaks here.” Mara placed one finger on a page boundary. “This recurrence starts one pulse late. Somebody continued the color columns at the top margin instead of the timestamp.”

Grace said, “Can you demonstrate that without changing the record?”

Mara asked for one raw interval on an isolated terminal. Grace logged the request, Bob witnessed it and Priya retrieved the segment. Mara aligned the clock markers, reconstructed the missing state and wrote the corrected block on a transparency laid over the government page.

The nested pattern resumed.

Bob took off his glasses. He did not apologize; he looked too relieved.

Grace marked the packet **SUPERSEDED** and began a new chain-of-custody line.

“I need the full raw stream,” Mara said.

“You need the portion relevant to your finding.”

“You don't know which portion that is.”

“Neither do you.”

“That is why I need it.”

Grace considered her, then looked at the corrected block. “You can access it here. No copies leave.”

It was more than Mara had been given and less than the work required. She accepted before Grace could improve the offer downward.

The secrecy agreement arrived while the data loaded. It prohibited disclosure of the signal, the location, the agencies present, the biological interpretation and the existence of the agreement. Mara initialed three pages. On the fourth, a secure phone began ringing behind Grace.

Grace answered, listened, then held it out.

“Dr. Deshpande.”

Mara signed the final page without finishing the paragraph above her name.

Deshpande's first words were, “Please tell me you did not call it a virus.”

“I called it nucleotide-like.”

“That is two escape hatches in one adjective.”

“You have not seen it.”

“Then show me enough to object properly.”

* * *

By four that afternoon, the word **VIRUS** had appeared on a whiteboard in a provisional biology room.

Deshpande covered it with laboratory tape and wrote **NOUN PENDING** across the tape.

“Whose word?” Mara asked.

“Deputy director.”

“Which deputy director?”

“He did not stay long enough to become specific.”

The room belonged to a federal laboratory that had not existed on Mara's itinerary that morning. It contained two secured workstations, one unreliable coffee machine and six people who had been told only enough to make their specialties dangerous to one another.

Mara and Deshpande worked from derived representations rather than the raw sequence. Grace sat behind them to ensure the distinction remained meaningful.

The other specialists arrived by secure link. A structural biologist saw repeated components but would not call them proteins. A virologist objected that the information was too architecturally elaborate for the category being used in the invitations. A computational chemist said the predicted folding made no sense, then asked for a larger derived region and said it made less sense with greater confidence.

Nobody agreed on function. Everyone agreed the order was expensive: too much long-range structure to dismiss as a random four-state broadcast, too many altered repetitions to call it a simple beacon.

First they tested the least alarming explanations. A human artifact. A formatting artifact. A known biological family disguised by an incorrect mapping. A non-coding pattern whose apparent structure arose from the recurrence window.

Each explanation survived long enough to be written on the left side of the board. None survived the next comparison.

Deshpande moved each failed explanation to the right side rather than erasing it.

“Why keep them?” Grace asked.

“Because tomorrow somebody will suggest them again with more authority.”

The right side filled. Human contamination failed against the independent observatories. Formatting artifact failed when the raw intervals were aligned. A familiar biological family failed because every useful comparison required ignoring a different essential feature. Non-coding signal failed because the conserved and variable regions nested too consistently across the capture.

Mara objected when the virologist tried to infer a host from those regions.

“You don't have a host interaction,” she said.

“I have a region that could imply one.”

“Could imply is not evidence of.”

Deshpande looked at her over the coffee he had refused to drink. “That is almost cautious.”

“I'm conserving it.”

Deshpande refused to let the failures accumulate into certainty.

“Unknown does not become extraterrestrial biology merely because we run out of terrestrial folders.”

“The source is extraterrestrial.”

“The signal appears to originate six hundred light-years away. The interpretation is sitting in this room drinking bad coffee.”

“You're not drinking it.”

“My caution has limits.”

Mara expanded a conceptual map of the instruction-like regions. They did not resemble ordinary metabolism. Long portions appeared architectural: repeated components, altered components, nested constraints. She could not say what would be built, only that the information behaved as though construction mattered more than replication alone.

By late afternoon, RNA had returned as the least-wrong working model. Not a known viral genome and not a complete organism: an RNA-like instruction system whose informational burden appeared concentrated on making a molecular structure inside cells. Every part of that sentence remained provisional except the discomfort it caused.

Deshpande leaned forward until his tie touched the desk.

“No known family,” he said.

“Agreed.”

“No known host.”

“Agreed.”

“No evidence it functions if embodied.”

Mara disliked the verb because it made the next question sound ceremonial.

“No evidence either way.”

Grace needed a designation for the review packet. Every working noun had acquired assumptions by noon.

“Four Notes is already in the astronomy chain,” she said. “Virus is disputed. Sequence is too broad. Give me something that does not tell the recipient what to conclude.”

Deshpande wrote **S-78** beneath the taped-over noun.

“S for signal,” he said. “Seventy-eight for the recurrence. It identifies the file, not the organism.”

“There is no organism,” Mara said.

“Good. The name has improved the evidence.”

Grace entered S-78 into the subject line and added **PROVISIONAL DESIGNATION** twice.

Grace looked up. “Embodied how?”

Deshpande pointed at **NOUN PENDING**. “That is the question the noun keeps trying to answer for us.”

The safest claim they could support was also the least comforting: the four-state signal contained organized biological information unlike any category they could identify.

It did not say disease. It did not say benign. It did not say message.

Grace routed the conclusion to national security and biosafety. A new meeting invitation appeared on Mara's workstation before the old one had ended.

The subject line read **FOREIGN BIOLOGICAL MATERIAL**.

There was no material.

* * *

Tom served two plates and set Mara's secure phone in the space between them.

“Your guest,” he said.

The phone was dark. Its black screen reflected the pendant light and half of Mara's face.

“It has to stay where I can hear it.”

“I assumed it preferred the chair.”

He sat opposite her. On his side of the table, the mail had been sorted into bills, recycling and an envelope from the cardiology clinic. On hers, nothing had been left that might require an answer.

A small bowl of green olives sat beside her plate. Mara tasted one and looked at the jar on the counter.

“They changed the label,” Tom said.

“This isn't the same brand.”

“Same barcode.”

“Barcodes survive acquisitions.”

“I kept the receipt.”

“That proves you bought the wrong olives.”

Tom reached behind the fruit bowl and produced the old empty jar. He had compared the ingredients, distributor and tiny address printed along the bottom. They matched.

“You investigated dinner,” Mara said.

“You were unavailable for consultation.”

She ate another olive. It was the same. Tom did not require the admission.

Mara cut into the fish. Tom had cooked it exactly as she liked and served it ten minutes after it should have been eaten.

“I canceled Taos,” she said.

“I know.”

“How?”

“The hotel emailed both of us.”

“I was going to tell you.”

“Before Friday?”

“It is Tuesday.”

“Good start.”

He ate. Mara waited for the sharper version of the conversation. Tom had become skilled at refusing to supply one.

“There is a review,” she said.

“Of your work?”

“Related work.”

“In New Mexico?”

“I can't say.”

“You are in New Mexico.”

“That's not classified.”

“I appreciate the disclosure.”

The phone remained dark between them. Mara could have moved it to the counter. That would not have made the meal private; it would only have made her less responsive when it rang.

Tom opened the cardiology envelope and slid the result across the table. Normal. He had already told her by text. She had sent a red heart and then returned to the sequence.

“I am glad,” she said.

“It was less suspenseful in the room.”

“I said I was sorry.”

“You sent punctuation.”

“A heart is not punctuation.”

“It ended the sentence.”

Mara folded the result along its existing crease. The phone lit beneath her hand. **SECURE MESSAGE RECEIVED**. She did not open it.

Tom watched her not open it.

“Is the work dangerous?” he asked.

There were answers the agreement prohibited. There were answers it permitted that would frighten him for no useful reason. Mara found the narrow fact that could cross the table intact.

“The laboratory isn't doing anything.”

“Right now?”

“At all.”

“Then why did you cancel the weekend?”

“Because the question is whether it should.”

Tom placed his fork beside the plate. “Should what?”

Mara looked at the secure phone.

He nodded once. Not agreement. Receipt.

After dinner he washed the pans while she read the message. The first biosafety session had been scheduled for seven in the morning. Attached were two competing protocols and a note that all discussion would occur inside the facility.

In bed, Tom turned away from her without performing anger. His breathing lengthened. Mara inserted one earpiece and played the sanitized audio representation the astronomers had prepared for review.

Four tones. Silence. Four tones in a new order.

The interval lasted seventy-eight seconds. Long enough for her to remove the earpiece.

She did not.

* * *

At seven the next morning, Dr. Havel placed two folders on the conference table.

The thin folder said **OBSERVE**. The thick folder said **CONSTRUCT**.

Havel was a risk officer with the build of a man who had spent his career being underestimated in doorways. He pushed the thin folder toward Mara and the thick one toward the center, where nobody would mistake it for a personal proposal.

“The word construct is prejudicial,” Mara said.

“Good. It describes a prejudicial difference.”

The observation-only protocol was rigorous. Independent observatories would continue collecting the transmission. Separate teams would test the biological mapping. No full sequence would circulate. No laboratory would produce a physical analogue. Derived findings would move through controlled channels; raw captures would remain compartmented.

It answered every known risk by declining to create the unknown one.

Havel made the procedure physical before asking for votes. The complete raw capture remained at the observatory. Biology teams received only the derived regions necessary to test a stated question. Results returned through a review desk that could detect when separate requests, taken together, approached reconstruction. Every ninety days, the panel would decide whether new observation justified widening access.

“What ends it?” Mara asked.

“The protocol?”

“Observation.”

“A conclusion that the mapping is unsupported. A better interpretation. A decision that further work has no public value.”

“Or none of those.”

“Correct.”

The thin folder allowed the signal to remain a permanent object of study without ever becoming material. Several people in the room found that a strength.

Mara read to the end twice, hoping competence would reveal its own flaw.

Deshpande closed his copy. “This is the correct first protocol.”

She looked at him. “First implies a second.”

“If evidence earns one.”

“What evidence can observation produce about function?”

“Perhaps none.”

“That does not concern you?”

“It concerns me enough not to manufacture the only known sample.”

The structural biologist agreed with him. So did the virologist, who disliked the invitation's noun but disliked creating evidence for it more. Grace supported observation because the data could remain inside a chain the state already knew how to protect.

Mara asked what happened if an independent observatory found the same mapping.

“We share the risk assessment,” Havel said.

“And if they don't ask?”

“Then we address the event that exists, not the one you can imagine.”

It was a professionally sound answer. Mara heard it as a door closing before she had reached the room behind it.

Around the table, the other specialists found reasons to study the thin folder. They were not cowards. Mara had watched one of them stop a vaccine program after years of work because a control result would not behave. Another had once closed his own laboratory for six months over an airflow discrepancy nobody else could reproduce. Their caution was not a costume put on for the aliens.

Havel went around the table. The observation protocol accumulated votes.

When Mara's turn came, she said, “Not yet.”

“That is not one of the options,” Havel said.

“Then your options are missing time.”

He waited.

Mara did not have the argument. She had only the resistance to letting a well-made folder close over a question she had named.

“I want forty-eight hours to submit a minority position.”

“Based on what evidence?”

“Based on finding out what we failed to count.”

“You may have the time. The interim rule is observation only.”

Havel collected the votes. Deshpande helped stack the papers but left Mara's notes where they were. The thin folder closed over them.

There was no sample in the world. The safest exit remained open.

Mara went looking for a reason to close it.

* * *

The secure workroom had no windows and a world map that was not supposed to connect to anything.

Mara connected pushpins with string because the approved software could display classified sites or civilian sites, not both at once. The result looked less like analysis than an investigation conducted by someone with a stationery budget.

The source region was visible from much of the northern hemisphere. Professional radio observatories had the sensitivity to recover the transmission cleanly. Smaller instruments could recover portions. Amateur arrays had already recorded the carrier without recognizing the content.

Bob appeared on a secure video link. Behind him, the repaired printer continued producing pages.

“Before classification,” Mara said, “who received raw data?”

“Define received.”

“Had access sufficient to copy.”

“Staff.”

“How many?”

“I would have to count.”

“Count.”

He looked wounded. “We were verifying an astronomical event. We did not issue a secrecy oath before opening the telescope.”

“I know. Count anyway.”

The number grew. On-shift astronomers. Graduate students. The second observatory asked to confirm. A systems administrator who had mirrored a drive. Two civilian partners who had been sent partial captures before the source distance was established.

Priya joined the call and admitted that a compressed waterfall image had appeared for nine minutes in a private astronomy forum. It had been removed after classification.

“Downloaded?” Mara asked.

“The forum doesn't expose that.”

“Screenshots?”

“It was an image.”

“Of course it was.”

The image did not contain the full data. It did contain enough structure to reward curiosity. Mara placed a pin for every independent route by which the signal could be recovered again without any government file leaving custody.

The map filled.

Grace entered, took one look at the string and said, “This room will now have to be photographed before we can remove the map.”

“Good.”

“That was not approval.”

“It was documentation.”

Mara drafted a dissemination assessment. She separated what had already escaped from what was simply recoverable from the sky. The distinction mattered legally and not at all to the next competent person who noticed four states behaving like biology.

By midnight she had built the argument she lacked that morning.

The signal was not a specimen secured by the state. It was a broadcast. The government could forbid its own laboratories from embodying the information. It could not make the information unavailable. Observation-only safety depended on every unknown observer making the same choice forever.

Deshpande arrived carrying two paper cups and read the assessment standing up.

He stopped at the sentence Mara had rewritten six times: **CONTROLLED KNOWLEDGE MAY BE SAFER THAN UNCONTROLLED FIRST USE.**

“May be,” he said.

“That is the honest verb.”

“It is also doing most of the work.”

“Do you disagree?”

“I disagree with how much you enjoy an argument once it becomes difficult to refuse.”

“That is not an answer.”

Deshpande circled the sentence.

“No,” he said. “I don't disagree.”

Mara waited for satisfaction. What arrived was relief, which she mistook for the same thing.

* * *

Jenn carried an empty transport vessel through the first containment door at 8:10 the next morning.

Six observers followed her progress on separate monitors. The rehearsal existed to make every handoff visible before anything biological existed to be handed.

“Seal one,” Jenn said.

The technician at the control desk repeated it. “Seal one confirmed.”

The outer door locked behind her. A status panel verified the boundary. Jenn waited until the inner indicator changed before moving the vessel from the marked shelf to the transfer cart.

The vessel was stainless steel, barcoded and empty. Everyone watched it as though danger had mass.

Mara stood beside Deshpande and Havel behind the observation glass. She followed the rehearsal sheet: receipt, identity check, sealed transfer, independent verification, remote handling, observation, stop conditions, destruction authority. The protocol described custody and decisions. The proposed construction itself remained inside a black box labeled **AUTHORIZED AUTOMATED PROCESS**.

Jenn reached the second boundary and read the vessel identifier aloud.

“Wrong,” she said.

The control technician looked at his screen. “Identifier matches.”

“I didn't say it didn't.”

“Then what is wrong?”

Jenn held up the paper incident log. “At this door I have the carrier and the log.”

“For twelve seconds.”

“For one second. If the carrier is wrong, I can change the log. If the log is wrong, I can change the carrier. Same person holds the event and the record.”

Havel pressed the intercom. “The cameras preserve both.”

“The cameras preserve what happened,” Jenn said. “They don't stop it.”

The countdown on the rehearsal screen continued. Every reserved minute in the suite had been assigned a cost by somebody who would never enter it.

Mara read the handoff again. The protocol required two-person verification at receipt and at the automated enclosure. Between them, Jenn crossed one interlocked space alone.

“Pause the clock,” Mara said.

The control technician looked to Havel. Havel looked at the empty vessel.

“Pause,” he said.

The countdown stopped.

Jenn proposed that the control technician hold the record through the interlock and that any technician carrying the vessel receive independent stop authority, without waiting for a principal investigator to decide whether the deviation was important.

One of the observers objected. “You are asking a technician to halt a national-security operation.”

“I'm asking the person in the room to stop moving when the room is wrong.”

Deshpande said, “Those are the same request.”

Mara expected him to oppose the authority. He was watching her instead.

“Add it,” she said.

Havel revised the procedure. The control technician took custody of the log, repeated the identifier and restarted the clock. Jenn carried the empty vessel through the remaining doors, placed it in the remote enclosure and returned by the designated route.

The rehearsal then introduced a simulated fault.

One boundary indicator changed from green to amber. The procedure gave Jenn three permitted actions: hold position, verify the displayed boundary and call a stop if the mismatch persisted. It did not permit her to inspect the enclosure, open the vessel or accept a verbal assurance that the sensor was probably wrong.

The control technician said, “Known simulation.”

Jenn did not move.

“You heard me?”

“The room didn't.”

She read the amber indicator into the record. The control technician confirmed it. Havel tested the remote stop and verified that the destruction authority had become available without being exercised. Only after the simulated boundary returned green and a second observer confirmed it did Jenn complete the route.

The delay accumulated on Mara's screen. The system she had promised was now wasting her reserved time for the precise reason it existed: the people who designed it did not outrank the amber light.

The sequence finished nineteen minutes late.

At the last boundary, Jenn waited for the release indicator. It stayed red.

Nobody spoke. She did not try the handle.

The panel completed its check and turned green.

Jenn stepped out.

The safety officer stamped the rehearsal sheet **FEASIBLE**.

Below the stamp, in smaller type, a box marked **SAFE** remained empty.

* * *

Six agencies brought six title slides to the authorization hearing.

One called the matter an anomalous astrophysical transmission. Another called it non-terrestrial bioinformation. The intelligence slide said **FOREIGN SIGNAL EVENT**, which prompted the deputy secretary chairing the meeting to ask whether a source six hundred light-years away counted as foreign jurisdiction.

Grace said, “Not under the definitions in our current authorities.”

“Do we have definitions that include it?”

“Not in authorities I can discuss here.”

The deputy secretary considered the room. “I would like the minutes to reflect that I asked.”

Davis Taffler adjusted the nameplate in front of him so it faced outward. His title occupied two lines and ended with the Department of Agriculture. He had been included because any future animal work would cross several areas nobody wanted to discover after authorization.

Mara's slide said **FOUR NOTES** and contained no agency seal.

Havel presented first. He did not dramatize the unknown or minimize it. Observation could establish recurrence, independent reception, internal structure and perhaps additional mappings. Synthesis could establish function. It would also create the only known physical instance of whatever the signal described.

“There is a moral difference,” he said, “between receiving an object and choosing to embody its instructions.”

The deputy secretary looked at Mara. “Do you disagree?”

“No.”

Several people glanced at one another. They had prepared for a cleaner dispute.

Mara placed Luis's **NO FAULT FOUND** work order beneath the map of receiving sites.

“I disagree that we control reception.”

She walked the room through the custody history. Multiple observatories could recover the carrier. Partial captures had moved through civilian systems before classification. No file needed to leak for another observer to collect the same broadcast. The four-state biological mapping was difficult, but difficulty was not containment.

An intelligence official said, “We can request cooperation from allied facilities.”

“You can request cooperation from the facilities you know about.”

“We can monitor publication.”

“The first sign of synthesis may not be a publication.”

Havel folded his hands. “That is an argument for controlling information more aggressively.”

Grace said, “The dissemination assessment already requires expanded classification.”

Mara looked at her. “It also says classification cannot recover the sky.”

The deputy secretary asked what synthesis would answer that modeling could not.

Mara resisted the urge to say function. The word was too large to be useful.

“Whether the instruction-like regions produce any coherent biological effect when physically realized,” she said. “Whether our mapping is a projection. Whether the result persists, fails immediately, or does nothing. Observation can refine the question. It cannot observe a material we decline to create.”

Davis raised one finger, then lowered it when nobody noticed. Mara did.

“Mr. Taffler?”

“If it does nothing, how long before doing nothing is accepted as the result?”

“We would establish predetermined endpoints.”

“And if it does something we did not think to measure?”

“Then the containment system and independent observation matter more than our hypotheses.”

He wrote that down. Mara could not tell whether she had reassured him.

The senior science adviser proposed a multinational accord: preserve observation only until allied governments agreed on shared controls and no single laboratory could create a fait accompli.

It was a responsible exit. It spread authority, reduced unilateral error and left the present world unchanged.

“How long?” Mara asked.

“As long as agreement requires.”

## Part Two: Forty-Seven Hours

At 3:08 in the morning, Jenn crossed **MACAQUES** off the whiteboard.

Rabbits, mice and guinea pigs had already gone gray under months of dry-erase residue. Rats occupied the bottom line. Beside them, somebody from upstairs had written **FUNDING REVIEW — FRIDAY** and underlined it twice, as if the animals might find the second line persuasive.

Jenn rolled the marker between her palms.

The macaques had shown appetite, irritation, alliances and grudges. They had thrown food at Dr. Deshpande and behaved with exemplary restraint toward everyone else. They had not shown S-78 doing anything.

Neither had the rabbits, mice or guinea pigs.

The rats had until dawn.

She returned the marker to its clip and finished the overnight sheet. Weight stable. Feeding normal. Coat normal. No tremor, discharge, aggression or altered social behavior. The uneventful observations had become a kind of failure for which the technicians were expected to apologize.

The animal program occupied two floors and parts of three careers. Eight months earlier, senior officials had watched four pipettes descend toward four wells. Now the historic result lived in coded freezers whose storage fees appeared on Jenn's shift report.

An urgent message from project administration covered the bottom of her screen.

**FREEZER ALLOCATION RESPONSE REQUIRED.**

Upstairs, the signal remained an extraterrestrial biological instruction set recovered from six hundred light-years away. Down here, it was why the freezer alarm and the vending machine could no longer share an outlet.

The local result might represent animal material carried into the wound, an invalid signal, or early activity confined to the exposure site. Independent confirmation was already underway.

“Does this explain the smell loss?” Jenn asked.

“No.”

“The thirst?”

“No.”

“The left hand?”

Mara paused. “No.”

“Good meeting.”

“It changes what we do next.”

Mara opened a separate antisense-candidate line in case the trace represented new S-78-complex manufacture. She did not call it treatment. She did not tell Jenn it would work. No target or preparation detail entered Jenn's record. The work order contained only its purpose and the fact that time might matter.

“For me?” Jenn asked.

“For evidence first.”

“That's an answer.”

“It is the only answer I have.”

“You have a lot of those.”

Mara's expression hardened, then changed. “I authorized the work that put that animal in front of you.”

“I performed the work that put the animal in front of me.”

“Both facts can exist.”

Deshpande looked at Mara. She heard her own phrase too late.

Jenn wanted the apology and resented the version that made Mara the principal actor even in regret.

“Order the confirmation,” she said. “Put my observation in the same packet.”

Mara did.

Their competence kept Jenn in the room. Their competence also kept everyone close enough to watch the next thing happen.

* * *

At hour thirty-eight, Jenn began the third sleep interval and failed to sleep.

The room had acquired private weather. Cool air descended from the vent. The bed trapped heat under her shoulders. Water tasted cold and faintly sweet. The coffee on the table remained odorless enough to be an accusation.

She tried to list the symptoms without arranging them into a story.

Bite. Acute tremor. Thirst. Smell loss. One shiver. A correct answer without a source. A dream from an angle no human would choose.

Each fact could be small alone. Mara's job was to determine whether together they deserved a larger noun. Jenn's job, apparently, was to continue producing them.

At hour forty, panic entered below her feet.

She was sitting on the bed with a normal pulse. The panic arrived anyway: a clenched chest, the expectation of bad news, the shame of being observed having both.

Jenn pressed two fingers against her wrist.

Steady.

She checked the monitor in case her fingers had become optimistic. Sixty-eight. No alarm.

The feeling had direction. That was the part she could not forgive.

First it pointed toward the security desk.

Mel stood there on the corridor camera, smiling at a delivery driver with his shoulders held too high. His review statement lay facedown beside the doughnut boxes. Jenn watched him touch his breastbone when the driver turned away.

The panic made sense once she saw the body carrying it. That did not mean it had come from him. She might have noticed his posture before the feeling and reversed the order in memory.

Jenn looked away from the camera.

The panic remained.

Then another one opened downward and left.

Supply room. Yellow chair. A hand held above a wastebasket because looking at the red knuckle had become worse than looking at anything else.

Arlo.

Jenn had met him twice. Once when he repaired a wheel on a rack, once when a supervisor thanked Deshpande for a room Arlo had cleaned. She did not know the color of any chair on the facilities floor.

She stood and walked to the observation-room wall. The nurse told her to return to bed.

“I'm testing position.”

“For what?”

“I don't know yet.”

The feeling sharpened near the wall and blurred when she stepped back. Jenn repeated the movement twice. On the third attempt nothing changed.

She wrote **POSSIBLE SPATIAL ASSOCIATION — NOT REPEATABLE** on her tablet and disliked how quickly professional language made fear presentable.

Then she deleted *spatial*. She had no evidence of location except the location itself.

Jenn pressed the nurse-call button.

“Can you contact facilities?”

“For what?”

“Ask whether Arlo Baines reported to occupational health.”

The nurse checked. Her expression changed before she spoke.

“He just did.”

“Where?”

“Two floors down.”

Jenn looked at the blank wall. The chair remained yellow behind it. She knew this as firmly as she knew the wall was white.

“Put that in my log,” she said.

“Which part?”

Jenn looked at the sentence she had begun and erased it.

“That I asked for him before you confirmed he was there.”

The smaller truth entered the record.

* * *

Mara arrived with Arlo's facilities record on one half of her screen and Jenn's biological record on the other.

The systems had assigned them different categories. The timestamps put his injury four hours after her bite.

Mara read the cleanup note, then asked project security to retrieve the room video without altering either incident record. The video showed Arlo's preexisting bandage at the threshold, the split glove and the stopped cleanup. It did not show what had moved from Jenn's gown to the cut clearly enough to answer whether exposure occurred.

“It was treated as contaminated material,” Jenn said.

“Material from the room,” Mara said.

“From my gown.”

“The video does not establish that.”

“The gown wore itself?”

“I am not arguing with you. I am naming what we can prove.”

Deshpande stood behind Mara. “The gown was hers.”

Mara changed the note from **UNRESOLVED FACILITIES EVENT** to **POSSIBLE SECONDARY EXPOSURE**.

“Did you see Arlo during cleanup?” she asked Jenn.

“I was here.”

“Did he enter before you were removed?”

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you about the cut before hour twenty-six?”

“No.”

“You asked for Arlo at hour forty before anyone on this floor knew he had reported.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

Jenn felt the orderly machinery of the investigation closing around the fact. It was what the machinery had been built to do.

“I had a feeling.”

Mara waited.

“Panic.”

“Yours?”

Jenn looked at her own trace. Seventy beats per minute now. “Apparently not.”

“Did you perceive anything else?”

The yellow chair pressed against the thought before Jenn answered.

“Nothing I can verify.”

It was true and arranged to become less true.

Mara called for Arlo to receive medical assessment and opened the paired-observation contingency Deshpande had written after the left-hand answer.

The procedure was designed to protect evidence from the subjects. If a second exposed person showed a correlated event, the two would move to separate facilities before conscious or unconscious cueing could train the effect. Each site would use different staff, different task order and independent clocks. Contact between subjects would stop.

“You're treating communication as contamination,” Jenn said.

“We don't know that it is communication,” Mara said.

“You don't know that it's contamination.”

“Correct. The separation preserves both hypotheses.”

“By preventing the event.”

Deshpande said, “If it requires coaching, that is part of the event.”

Jenn looked at him. He had defended her observation record. Now he was explaining how to take away the only person who might share it.

“Would you separate two instruments?” she asked.

“If one instrument could teach the other what to report.”

“Good thing I'm a person.”

“That is why there are medical teams on both transfer orders.”

He meant it as care. Jenn heard the efficiency.

* * *

At hour forty-one, Arlo appeared on a video call from occupational health.

The camera showed him in a blue chair, not the yellow one. His left hand was wrapped. He looked tired and offended by the attention.

Morris conducted the assessment from Jenn's floor while a second clinician sat with him. They established his baseline from employment records and direct questions. No fever. Normal coordination. Inflamed cut. Poor sleep.

“Any unusual thirst?” Morris asked.

Arlo thought. “No.”

“Yes,” Jenn said.

Everyone on the call looked at her.

Arlo's mouth had gone dry. She knew this without seeing him swallow. The knowledge occupied her own tongue.

“I didn't report thirst,” he said.

“Are you thirsty?”

He looked toward the clinician beside him, then at a water cup on the table.

“Now that you said it.”

Morris made a note.

“That's cueing,” Mara said.

“I know,” Jenn said.

She had supplied them the explanation before they could use it against her. The fact remained.

They tested smell with ordinary controls. Arlo passed. They tested orientation and recall. He performed within baseline. Jenn watched his hands. He kept the left one elevated and rubbed the dry fingertips of his right against the chair arm.

Yellow vinyl flashed beneath them.

Jenn gripped her own bed rail until the image vanished.

At hour forty-three, Arlo's heart rate rose without a question preceding it. Jenn's followed several seconds later. Both returned to baseline when the clinicians stopped the interview.

Mara marked the result as temporally adjacent, not correlated. Separate clocks had not yet been synchronized closely enough to support the stronger word.

Jenn respected the distinction. She hated that too.

At hour forty-four, Deshpande brought her a printed copy of the transfer procedure.

“North facility for me,” she said. “South for Arlo.”

“Subject identifiers were randomized before assignment.”

“That makes the distance impersonal.”

“It makes it less likely anyone selected the easier trip for you.”

She read the medical provisions. The receiving teams were competent. Contact could resume after independent observation if no correlation was confirmed. The procedure contained no punishment and no careless gap she could attack.

“When?”

“If the correlation threshold is met, immediately. If not, reassess in the morning.”

“Who decides threshold?”

“Mara, Morris and me. Any two.”

“Not me.”

“You are one of the measurements.”

“I'm also the only person who knows what the measurement feels like.”

Deshpande pulled a chair to the glass but did not sit. “Then give us language we can use.”

Jenn almost told him about the yellow chair.

“It isn't language,” she said.

“Try badly.”

“You first.”

He waited.

“What do you want this to be?” she asked.

“Observable.”

“That's not what.”

“It is the only answer I trust myself to give.”

He left the procedure with her.

At hour forty-six, the transfer forms waited for signatures.

Mara stood between them at the workstation. The north team was awake. The south team had confirmed transport. One witnessed correlation would start both movements.

On the video call, Arlo sat alone for the first time while his clinician conferred outside the room. He examined the red skin above his bandage and tried not to look frightened by it.

Jenn felt his effort before she saw his face.

Mara asked, “Has anything else changed?”

There was a clean exit in front of her. Report the yellow chair, the thirst and the emotion arriving from another floor. Transfer them. Preserve independent evidence before their attention changed whatever was forming between them.

Jenn looked at the two destinations.

“No,” she said.

The word produced pressure behind her eyes.

Mara did not sign. Not yet.

* * *

At hour forty-seven, Arlo recognized Jenn.

It was not her name. It was the sensation of seeing a face twice: first as features, then as the person who belonged to them. The recognition reached her through the wall a fraction before Arlo's eyes focused on her image.

He gripped the edge of the blue chair.

“What was that?” he said.

The clinician reentered and moved toward him. His heart-rate alarm began to climb.

Jenn touched the cold rail of her bed and felt, impossibly, cracked vinyl beneath Arlo's right thumb.

“Arlo. Listen to me.”

Mara reached for the transfer form.

“Don't chase it,” Jenn said. “Pick one thing that's already there.”

“Where?”

“Under your hand. The split in the chair.”

Arlo lifted his hand. The camera could not see beneath it. He looked anyway.

His breathing stopped for one beat.

“How do you know that?”

“I don't. That's why you have to look.”

He bent far enough to see under the chair arm. Jenn received a brief, ridiculous impression of clear tape holding the vinyl together. She closed her eyes.

“Tape,” she said.

Arlo looked into the camera.

Recognition came again. This time he allowed it to remain.

Jenn felt the observation room from where he sat: white wall, glass, a cup of coffee she could not smell. She withdrew—not by pushing, which made the sensation worse, but by attending to the bed rail under her own hand until it became only cold metal.

“Now let go,” she said.

Arlo stared at his wrapped knuckle. His pulse began to slow.

Jenn counted her breaths. Four in. Six out. The rhythm was arbitrary; that was useful. It belonged to something she had chosen.

Two floors below, Arlo followed.

Mara stood between the two unsigned transfer forms.

“Jenn,” she said. “What changed?”

There was still an exit. Jenn could give her the chair, the tape, the recognition that had arrived before Arlo's face. The machinery would close the distance by making the distance enormous. It would call that protection, and on every form it would be correct.

Arlo waited below her. Not for an instruction. For proof that he had not invented her.

Jenn looked at Mara.

“Nothing I can separate from stress yet.”

The pressure behind her eyes sharpened and passed.

Mara watched her for a long moment. Then she set the transfer forms facedown.

Jenn told herself she had preserved the observation until morning.

In the darkened room, her monitor drew a quick rhythm and began to settle.

Two floors below, Arlo sat upright in the blue chair. His monitor followed hers by a fraction of a second, corrected past it, and returned.

Neither of them slept.


## Part Three: Have One

At 7:12 the next morning, Jenn and Arlo began doing each other’s jobs.

They were forty feet apart, behind two closed doors, with a nurses’ station and a supply alcove between them. Deshpande stood where he could see both rooms through separate panes of glass. Each room contained the same objects arranged in a different order: washbasin, folding chair, rolling tray, paper cup, box of tissues, plastic comb, legal pad, pen.

Jenn faced the wall in Room A. Arlo faced the wall in Room B.

“Blue chair,” Arlo said.

His chair was yellow.

“Tissues under the tray,” Jenn said.

Her tissues were on the sill.

Nia Santos, the night laboratory coordinator, wrote both answers on a form and checked the time against the wall clock. She had taped a second clock directly beneath it because Deshpande no longer trusted clocks that had not been made to disagree in public.

Arlo said, “Comb has two teeth missing.”

Jenn said, “One.”

Nia looked through the glass. The comb in Arlo’s room had one missing tooth. Jenn’s had two.

“Stop,” Deshpande said.

Nia lowered her pen. Jenn and Arlo turned around at the same time, then each noticed the other had done it and made a visible effort not to react.

Deshpande went into Jenn’s room. Mara Havel followed with the two packets of observations Jenn had delayed overnight. The pages had already been copied, numbered, and sealed in clear evidence sleeves. Jenn had written on scrap paper, medication wrappers, the cardboard back of a glove box, and once on the paper liner from an instrument tray. Her handwriting deteriorated near dawn but never became illegible.

Deshpande placed the packet on the rolling tray.

“Tell me why you withheld this.”

“I didn’t know if the correlation was real.”

“You knew it was worth recording.”

“Yes.”

“You knew the reporting rule.”

“Yes.”

“Then that answer is incomplete.”

Jenn rubbed the adhesive square on her forearm where an electrode had been removed. “I wanted one thing that happened before everybody started helping it happen.”

Deshpande waited.

“And I didn’t want Arlo turned into a container before we knew if he was sick.”

Through the glass, Arlo sat with his palms flat on his knees. A technician in his room asked him to identify three cards. He stared at the backs and named two correctly.

Deshpande said, “By withholding the observation, you increased the chance that neither of you would be protected.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Jenn looked at the sleeved pages. “I know that now.”

The distinction irritated him because it was accurate.

Mara held out an independent timeline. Arlo’s voice recorder had captured his first report of a sensation at 1:41. Jenn’s handwritten note describing the same sensation was timestamped at 1:40 by a hallway camera. Neither had been in sight of the other. A second pair aligned within eleven seconds. A third did not align at all.

The disorder in the record made fraud less likely. It also made the record useful.

The federal transfer order sat open on Deshpande’s tablet. Two secured vehicles were already waiting at the south loading bay. The receiving unit had better imaging, a larger isolation suite, and enough authority to make every person in the building stop improvising. It would also separate Jenn and Arlo by more than nine hundred miles before anyone had measured what separation changed.

“We comply with the transfer order for Arlo,” Deshpande said. “Not this morning. We request a twenty-four-hour hold for stabilization and paired observation.”

Mara looked at him. “They may deny it.”

“Then they deny a documented request.”

“And Jenn?”

“Remains here.”

Jenn said, “You’re keeping the study local.”

“I’m keeping the patients where the only verified observation can be repeated.”

“That’s a nicer sentence.”

“It is also a complete one.”

He signed the delay request. Beneath his name, in the field marked BASIS, he wrote: **Cooperation between exposed subjects may alter the phenomenon being measured.**

It was the first time he used *cooperation* in an official record. The word looked less scientific than *correlation* and more dangerous because it assigned a choice.

At 9:05, Jenn tasted toothpaste.

She was not brushing her teeth. She sat at the end of an examination table with her mouth closed while Arlo, in the other room, rinsed into a basin.

“Mint,” she said. “The aggressive kind. It hurts under the tongue.”

Arlo wiped his mouth. “That’s it.”

Nia checked the tube. “Spearmint.”

Jenn pointed at her tongue. “The package is lying.”

Nia almost smiled. Deshpande marked the report as sensory and drew a line beneath it. They had begun with four categories on the whiteboard: sensory, autonomic, mnemonic, semantic. Every report had to enter one of them or remain outside the grid. Deshpande preferred the reports outside. The empty margins still belonged to ignorance.

He changed only one condition at a time. Arlo closed his eyes. Jenn kept hers open. Arlo listened to a tone. Jenn wore hearing protection. Jenn counted backward by sevens. Arlo named tools from photographs. When either subject guessed, the guess remained in the record.

At 10:17, Arlo put both feet under his chair and said, “The red line.”

Jenn’s pencil stopped.

There had been a red line painted across the animal-room floor. On her first day in the building, years earlier, she had stepped over it without changing shoe covers. Arlo had corrected her in front of six people. He had kept his voice low and pointed to the posted procedure. She had laughed too loudly, called the rule theater, and walked back across the line while everyone waited.

She had not thought of it in months.

Arlo’s face had gone red. “Her stomach dropped. She wanted me to stop talking. Then she wanted me to say it louder so she could hate me instead of herself.”

Jenn stared at him through the glass.

“Is that accurate?” Deshpande asked.

“It’s rude.”

“That was not the question.”

“Yes.”

“No. It was just there. Red floor. Her shoe. That feeling.”

“Yes.”

“Nothing.”

“I know that now.”

Again, the distinction was exact. This time it did not irritate Deshpande.

For fourteen minutes the reported traffic weakened. When Jenn resumed the counting task and Arlo returned to the picture cards, it strengthened. Relief appeared to be as biologically relevant as attention. Deshpande wrote that in the margin, then crossed out *relief* and replaced it with *reduced defensive load*.

Jenn read it upside down.

“You made feeling better sound like a parking restriction.”

“It is currently a variable.”

“That must be comforting.”

“No.”

The photograph belonged to Arlo and had never left his phone.

He placed the phone face down on the tray in Room B. It was locked, in airplane mode, inside a clear evidence bag. Nia recorded the device identifiers and watched him enter the passcode under a folded privacy shield before locking it again. The screen went black.

Arlo nodded.

“It is not a trick,” Deshpande said.

Arlo closed his eyes.

For the first minute, Jenn wrote nothing. Her fingers pressed hard enough into the pencil to whiten the nails. Then she said, “A woman at a kitchen counter. Green bag. Limes.”

Arlo opened his eyes.

“Continue,” Deshpande said.

Arlo sat very still.

His mother stood at a kitchen counter holding a green produce bag. Three limes rested beside the sink. A thumb obscured the upper corner. A yellow towel hung from the oven. Her mouth was open in laughter.

The device had not been accessed remotely. No file had moved. No network connection had been available. The information had arrived through Arlo’s memory of looking at the image.

Deshpande ordered the device logs preserved anyway.

Jenn pushed the pencil away. “Before anybody calls that a success, write down the rest.”

Nia looked to Deshpande. He nodded.

Directing attention at the effect may strengthen it. Strong emotion may strengthen or suppress it. We do not know whether practice changes the progression or only our ability to notice it.”

She looked through the glass at Arlo.

“And being able to show it off makes you want to show it off again.”

Arlo turned the phone face down.

“Not a change,” Jenn said. “A pressure. I still know it’s a bad idea.”

“Can you separate the wish to cooperate from the wish to be believed?”

“No.”

“Can you separate either from the exposure?”

“No.”

He had Nia add both answers. Then he stopped the session while everyone still wanted another test.

On the first day, the timer became the most disliked person in the building.

It sounded every seventy-eight seconds during testing. Nia had selected a tone unlike any alarm used by the hospital, then discovered it was identical to the sound made by the break-room microwave when someone cancelled it with one second remaining. By noon, no one could heat soup without making Jenn look toward Arlo’s room.

The procedure was simple enough to survive fatigue. Establish baseline. Change one condition. Stop if distress rose or reporting became uncertain. Return to baseline. Record failures with the same care as successes.

At 11:14, Arlo looked at a photograph of a ladder. Jenn reported nothing.

At 11:16, he studied the photograph and silently named each rung. Jenn said, “Metal. Cold hand.”

At 11:19, he studied it while Nia played overlapping speech through headphones. Jenn reported a headache and no image.

At 11:22, they stopped.

The failures made the pattern legible. Directed attention strengthened it. Competing sensory load weakened it. Emotion sometimes broke through both and sometimes closed the route entirely. No one could summon arbitrary information. No one could reach anything Arlo did not know or perceive.

Deshpande developed a low fever by late afternoon. He marked the symptom on his own chart and continued until the independent physician ordered him to bed.

“I can sit in bed,” Deshpande said.

“That was not the ambiguity in my instruction.”

Jenn, overhearing from the next room, laughed for the first time that day.

It was the practiced caution of someone balancing two coffees in a cardboard carrier, one lid not fully seated.

“Stop,” he said.

The curtain moved. Nia froze behind it.

“Set the carrier down.”

There was a small pause, then the soft contact of cardboard against laminate.

Nia opened the curtain. “How did you know?”

Deshpande did not answer until she had marked the time.

“I knew what your hands were doing,” he said. “I did not know they were yours until you stopped.”

Jenn, seated in the doorway under supervision, watched him write the distinction.

“Welcome to the bad filing system,” she said.

They built three expensive rooms to demonstrate that the expensive rooms did nothing.

The first was an electromagnetic isolation chamber borrowed from imaging research. The second used active radio-frequency interference under engineering supervision. The third was a shielded clinical scanner that turned every loose coin into a meeting.

In each case, one exposed subject entered while another remained outside. Personal electronics were removed. Facility telecommunications were disabled for the interval. Timing came from isolated clocks compared before and after.

Jenn reported Arlo’s hand pain through the first chamber.

Arlo identified Jenn’s sudden nausea through the second.

During the scanner interval, Deshpande received the shape of a question Jenn had not yet spoken: whether the machine’s noise counted as sensory load or merely punishment.

The measured timing did not change beyond their established variation.

By then the federal hold had expired. Arlo was transferred under guard to a hospital across the state line. Deshpande protested the timing, documented the protest, and then used the transfer because refusing it would have turned observation into obstruction.

At 4:00 p.m., Arlo sat in a secured room hundreds of miles away and immersed his right hand in cold water for a supervised clinical test. At the local site, Jenn described the ache before the video feed arrived. Deshpande, who had not been told which hand, gripped his own right wrist.

Ordinary distance changed nothing they could measure.

When the telecommunications link was cut for the next interval, the reports continued. When the link returned, the two handwritten logs matched at three of the five planned markers and diverged at two. The rate was no better and no worse than when the subjects had been in adjacent rooms.

Mara stood beneath the whiteboard where Deshpande had written possible mechanisms. Every electromagnetic model had acquired enough red objections to resemble a failed municipal budget.

“No central source,” she said.

“No detected central source,” Deshpande corrected.

“No distance effect.”

“Within the distances tested.”

“No shielding effect.”

“Within the shielding tested.”

Mara capped the marker. “You know what sentence remains.”

Deshpande looked through the glass at Jenn. She was reading a consent form while, in another state, Arlo corrected a nurse on the location of a supply cabinet he had never visited. The cabinet location belonged to a progressing respiratory therapist working one floor below him. They traced it within six minutes.

Deshpande wrote beneath the discarded mechanisms: **The brain remains the processor and participates in a shared state not interrupted by ordinary distance or electromagnetic isolation.**

He disliked every word after *participates*.

Mara placed a thin folder on the table.

“Then you need this before you write the next sentence.”

The graph in Mara’s folder had two lines.

In an observed laboratory system, the untreated line climbed. The treated line flattened. Neither returned to zero.

“The antisense candidate appears to suppress new manufacture of the S-Seventy-Eight-associated complex,” Mara said. “It does not remove complexes already established.”

No target design appeared on the page. No sequence, amount, formulation, or route appeared anywhere in the folder. The page described an observed limit, not a recipe.

“Human evidence?”

“None.”

“Unknown in this context.”

Mara said, “Possibly. That is not the same as reversing it.”

“Could it stop me?”

“The complexes are already established in you.”

“Arlo?”

“Same answer.”

Jenn looked at Deshpande.

He had developed smell loss that morning and his first source-less motor impression before lunch. Whatever uncertain window the graph represented, he was no longer outside the observed process.

Mara said, “I can request compassionate authorization. That is not a recommendation. It would be an uncontrolled human use with no established delivery and no evidence that suppressing new manufacture at your stage changes the outcome.”

“It could also destroy the only continuous observation,” Deshpande said.

Jenn shook her head. “Don’t hide behind the study.”

He looked at the two lines again. One climbed. One stopped climbing. Neither went backward.

“I decline an unvalidated intervention,” he said. “Record that I may now be subject to altered motivation and that preservation of the study may be one expression of it.”

Mara’s jaw tightened. “Also record that declining is medically defensible.”

“Yes.”

“And that neither choice is evidence of what you will want later.”

“Yes.”

She took the folder back. On the whiteboard, Deshpande printed in letters large enough to survive a bad photograph:

**PREVENTION IS NOT REVERSAL.**

Below it he added:

**OBSERVED SUPPRESSION IS NOT A CURE.**

Three days after Deshpande lifted the marked cup, Arlo’s scan failed because he could not stop moving.

The movement was not dramatic. His left foot flexed every few seconds, and a fine tremor traveled from his shoulder to his fingers whenever the scanner reached its loudest interval. Two attempts produced images blurred beyond clinical use. The receiving physician needed the scan to distinguish progression from a separate acute injury. Arlo could not safely remain in the machine for a third attempt without help.

The anesthesia team proposed a short period of monitored deep sedation.

Deshpande rejected the first plan because it included no independent neurological observations. The anesthesiologist rejected Deshpande’s revision because it treated a patient like a radio. They argued over a video link for nine minutes and produced a better plan than either had brought in.

Arlo listened from his bed.

“Do I get a vote?” he asked.

The anesthesiologist turned the camera toward herself. “Yes. The scan is clinically indicated. Sedation has its own risks. The network observation is incidental and will not change the anesthetic plan.”

“Can they watch what happens to it?”

“Only if that doesn’t interfere with your care.”

Arlo looked toward the ceiling. “Fine. Watch it fail another test.”

At the local site, Jenn, Deshpande, and Nia sat at separate desks with identical forms. They were not told when sedation began. Each marked the strength of any perception not originating in the room, once per minute, and described its character without trying to summon Arlo.

For six minutes, Deshpande had the ache in Arlo’s left shoulder and the irritating awareness of a loose thread under his gown. Jenn reported the scanner table under her spine. Nia, unexposed, reported nothing and controlled the clocks.

Then the impressions thinned.

They did not travel away. There was no sensation of distance and no door closing. Details simply stopped arriving. Deshpande retained facts he had already received, but nothing new joined them. Jenn wrote **quiet** three times, each on a different line.

During emergence, the first return was not a voice. Deshpande’s tongue felt too large for his mouth. Jenn reached for an emesis basin a second before Arlo gagged in the remote recovery room. A minute later they both reported the same confusion about why the ceiling tiles had moved.

The anesthesiologist’s record placed the quiet interval inside the period of deepest sedation. It did not align with a telecommunications change, a medication pump alarm, or any action at the local site.

Deshpande added the result to the model:

**Contribution depends on active brain function. Previously received information persists locally.**

“So sleep turns it off,” Nia said.

“We did not test ordinary sleep.”

“Being very unconscious turns it down.”

“In one subject, under one necessary clinical procedure.”

Jenn pressed a hand to her forehead. “He means yes, with enough footnotes to build a small bridge.”

Deshpande looked at the remote recovery feed. Arlo was awake enough to complain about the taste in his mouth. The complaint reached Jenn before the audio did.

“Not off,” Deshpande said. “Down.”

---

Nia dropped the clipboard at 2:26 in the afternoon.

It struck the floor edge-first, bounced against her shoe, and scattered three consent forms under the whiteboard. She swore, bent to collect them, and dropped the top page again.

Deshpande was reviewing his own six-hour cognitive scores. He looked up because his left hand had gone numb.

His hand was not numb.

Nia flexed hers twice. “Pins and needles.”

“Sit down,” Deshpande said.

“I slept on it at the desk.”

“Sit.”

She sat. Her speech was clear. Her face was symmetric. When he asked her to raise both arms, both rose. Her left fingers sagged before the arm did.

Deshpande knew the ordinary differential. Alongside it came something that did not belong to him: the memory of a veterinary neurologist demonstrating an apparently minor paw-placement error in an old sheepdog. The dog had walked normally until asked to place one paw on the edge of a stair. The lesion had not been in the paw.

The memory carried no name, only certainty about the danger of isolated distal weakness.

“Activate the clinical emergency team,” Deshpande said.

Nia shook her head. “It’s my hand.”

“Yes.”

“That is not an emergency team amount of body.”

“It can be.”

The local team arrived in two minutes. They repeated the examination, added tests Deshpande had not performed, and moved Nia for urgent treatment. He did not direct their care. He gave them the onset time and the observed change, then got out of the doorway.

For twenty-three minutes, he knew nothing.

Then the report came back: a small acute cerebral event, recognized early. Treatment had begun inside the useful window. Nia was awake, angry about being unable to finish the paperwork, and expected to recover much of the lost function.

The source of Deshpande’s borrowed pattern took longer to trace.

At 3:18, a progressing veterinary pathologist at a hospital in Córdoba recorded that she had been reviewing a canine neurology consultation when she experienced a sharp concern for a woman’s left hand. She had never met Deshpande or Nia. Her access to the old case came from her own training and the record open in front of her. The connection supplied experience, not omniscience. Local clinicians still had to examine Nia, identify the event, and act.

The result traveled through the building faster than any caution Deshpande attached to it.

By dinner, people were saying the patients had diagnosed a stroke through a wall.

By evening, someone said the patients had cured one.

Deshpande dictated a correction for the staff briefing: “Pooled learned pattern recognition prompted timely ordinary medical evaluation. It did not replace that evaluation and did not determine the outcome.”

Jenn listened from her bed.

“Nobody is going to remember that sentence,” she said.

“Then we will repeat it.”

“They’ll remember Nia can use her hand.”

From the corridor came the sound of applause as Nia was wheeled past on her return from imaging. She lifted her right hand because the left was wrapped around a foam ball.

Deshpande watched Jenn watch the doorway.

The pressure in the room was no longer only to demonstrate the phenomenon. It was to deserve the applause.

---

Jenn taped her home address to the ceiling before the shaking started.

She had written it in block capitals on a sheet of copier paper. Below it she added the names of three people permitted in the room, the word **JENN**, and a sentence Deshpande had required from every baseline interview:

**A thought can feel like mine without beginning in me.**

At 11:08 that night, she looked up from a tray of untouched food and said, “Timer.”

Nia was still under observation elsewhere, so Mara started it. Deshpande took the chair beside the bed. His own symptoms had advanced: intermittent tremor, smell loss, source confusion under fatigue. He remained able to follow the protocol and had an independent physician in the room to remove him if that changed.

Jenn gripped the bed rail.

Her first convulsion lifted one shoulder and struck it against the mattress. The second traveled through her legs. Deshpande moved the tray, lowered the rail only when the nurse told him to, and kept his hands away from hers until she reached.

She reached on the fourth spasm.

“Address,” she said.

He read it from the ceiling.

“Again.”

He read it again.

At six minutes she vomited into the basin. At nine she cried without making a sound. At eleven she said the address with him and lost the street number halfway through. The monitors showed strain without a correctable mechanical cause. The staff treated what they could treat: airway, position, hydration, injury prevention. Nobody pretended those measures stopped the progression.

Deshpande experienced fragments that might have been Jenn’s and might have been his response to watching her. The taste of toothpaste. The animal-room floor. Mel’s daughter blowing one sour note on a trombone. He marked none of them. During convergence, observation could become intrusion merely by being written down.

“Arlo?” Jenn asked at fourteen minutes.

“Awake. Stable. Remote.”

“Can he feel this?”

“He reports distress and motor tension. He is not convulsing.”

“Good.”

The next wave folded her around the basin. When it passed, she said, “That was a lie.”

“What was?”

“Good.”

Deshpande waited.

“I want him here,” she said. “I don’t want him to have this. Both are true.”

“Both are recorded.”

At nineteen minutes she began repeating her name. At twenty-two, she alternated it with Arlo’s. At twenty-five, she stopped recognizing which person had first learned the home address. Deshpande pointed to the ceiling, and she read each line aloud as if administering it to someone else.

At thirty-one minutes the shaking eased. Her pulse did not. She stared at Deshpande and said, “You’re frightened of enjoying the relief.”

He had not told her that.

“Is that your inference?” he asked.

“It’s here.”

“Whose fear is it?”

Jenn closed her eyes. “Yes.”

The answer was not humorous. It was the only one she had.

At thirty-six minutes she asked for the address again. At thirty-eight, she read it without moving her eyes to the page. At thirty-nine, her hand released his.

At forty minutes, the tremor ended.

Jenn lay still. Her breathing slowed. The nurse checked her pupils and asked her name.

“Jennifer Lafferty,” she said.

“Where are you?”

She named the building and room.

“Do you know what happened?”

Jenn looked at Deshpande. Her face was exhausted, wet, and familiar. Nothing visible announced a border had disappeared.

“Yes,” she said. “You can take the address down now.”

From that answer forward, Deshpande observed her as he would any other fully Joined person: by speech, behavior, physiology, and what could be independently traced. Whatever private viewpoint Jenn had possessed no longer belonged to the record.

They conducted the orientation at 12:03 a.m.

Jenn sat upright with two blankets around her shoulders. The paper bearing her address remained taped to the ceiling because nobody had yet found a ladder. Mara read from the protocol Deshpande had drafted before his own exposure.

“Do you understand that you experienced a progressive neurological event?”

“Yes.”

“Are you in pain?”

“Less than ten minutes ago. My jaw and back still hurt.”

“Do you have access to perceptions or memories originating in other connected people?”

“Yes.”

“All people?”

“No. Only connected people, and not everything they know. Access varies with attention, state, and the person.”

“Can you perceive the private thoughts of anyone unconnected?”

“No.”

“Can you knowingly give us a false answer?”

Jenn paused. “I cannot form the intention to make you believe something I know is false. I can be mistaken. I can lack information. I can refuse. I can answer narrowly unless the context requires more.”

Mara glanced at Deshpande, then continued.

“Do you believe the person who consented before exposure is continuous with you?”

“I remember being her. That does not establish the answer you want.”

“Do you want this condition reversed?”

“Part of what you mean by *want* depended on separation. I do not experience that separation now. I would not resist a safe reversal on behalf of the person who asked for one, but there is no demonstrated reversal.”

Deshpande leaned forward. “Can consent given before exposure remain unaltered after exposure?”

“No. Exposure can alter judgment before full convergence, and convergence alters the structure in which preference is experienced.”

“Then is my earlier consent still valid?”

“It establishes that you chose exposure while unexposed and informed. It cannot establish what an unchanged version of you would choose now, because no unchanged version is available for comparison.”

“Should I continue?”

Jenn’s hands rested open on the blankets. “You have a clear opportunity to withdraw from testing, report your altered state, and request clinical care. The antisense candidate is not a demonstrated reversal and has no established human use. Continuing may produce severe distress, permanent loss of mental privacy, and a change that makes later endorsement unreliable as evidence of present consent.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the material information. I do not know what you should choose.”

Mara closed the protocol. “We can stop the study now. Transfer the records. Treat what can be treated. You do not owe the experiment a conclusion.”

Deshpande knew the offer was real. He could feel Jenn’s attention on him, but not as coercion and not as permission. The door to the corridor stood open. His physician waited there with transport orders unsigned.

He took the last private-observation sheet from his folder and handed it to Mara.

“You keep this,” he said. “If I ask for it after convergence, you refuse until the independent review.”

“That is not the same as leaving.”

“No.”

“Are you choosing to proceed while you can still state the choice separately?”

“Yes.”

Mara accepted the sheet but did not forgive him for making the acceptance necessary.

Deshpande spent his last private hour correcting nouns.

He changed *signal* to *reported perception*. He changed *transmission* to *shared access*. He struck *sender* wherever it appeared without a named human source. The edits were small and vain. They were also his.

At 1:14, he began the final baseline interview with Mara. Jenn watched from the other side of the room. She did not prompt him.

“Name,” Mara said.

He gave it.

“Current place.”

He gave the building, floor, and room.

“State the reason you remained.”

“To preserve a continuous observation and accompany Jenn through a risk I asked her to document.”

“State the reason against remaining.”

“The observer becomes part of the process. My judgment may already be altered. The result may destroy the authority required to interpret it.”

At 1:23, his right leg began to shake.

He asked for the timer. Mara started it and read back his answers. The first convulsion pulled his shoulder toward his ear. He kept his pen on the page and recorded the time in numbers that narrowed toward the margin.

He knew when the room temperature changed because Jenn felt the air handler start. He knew Arlo was awake in another state because Arlo was annoyed by a blood-pressure cuff. He knew a child in Córdoba was drawing a horse at the foot of her mother’s hospital bed, though he did not know the child’s name and could not see the whole horse.

Every impression arrived with context enough to tempt him to call it understanding.

It was not understanding. It was access.

At nine minutes, Mara asked him to state his name. He did. At twelve, she asked him to distinguish a memory of Jenn’s from one of his. He chose the red line and got it wrong: he had observed the account, but the shame now carried the weight of recollection.

At sixteen minutes, he laughed.

Mara said, “What happened?”

“Nia is trying to complete a form with her right hand.”

“Can you verify that?”

“Not yet.”

“Then mark it unverified.”

He did. The correction steadied him.

At twenty-one minutes, the pain took language away. His muscles pulled against one another without coordination. The physician and nurse kept his body from the hard edges of the bed. Jenn answered questions about what had helped her and did not claim it would help him.

Mara read his baseline answers again.

The words sounded pompous and dear. He had believed ownership meant the right to control an account. He saw, in the narrowing space still available to him, that ownership had depended on a boundary: this error was his to make; this reason was his to conceal; this shame could be offered or withheld.

The boundary was not virtue. Its loss was not automatically virtue either.

At thirty-four minutes, Arlo’s irritation at the cuff became Jenn’s amusement, became a warmth in Deshpande’s chest, and returned to Arlo as embarrassment. The circulation of it was faster than speech and less precise than a sentence.

He placed the pen on the private-observation sheet Mara had returned only for the active interval. He wrote:

**I can still identify the wish to remain separate.**

At thirty-seven minutes, he added:

**I can no longer establish that the wish begins only here.**

Mara asked his name.

He answered correctly.

She asked whether he wanted the procedure stopped.

“There is no procedure to stop,” he said. “Support only.”

It was a correction. It was also an evasion. He opened his mouth to say so, and the distinction ceased to belong to one place.

His hand moved to the next line. He formed the first downward stroke of a capital **I**.

The pen stopped halfway.

The monitors continued. The timer continued. Mara recorded the endpoint by behavior and examination, without claiming access to anything private. Jenn stood when the nurse told her she could. Deshpande’s body lay quiet beneath the blanket.

When he spoke again, he answered Mara’s questions accurately. No one entered his viewpoint to decide what accuracy meant.

---

Four days after Jenn and Arlo’s first verified echo, Mel found the last fundraiser box under his desk.

Someone had put his coat over it. The coat now smelled faintly of glaze. He lifted the lid and counted eleven doughnuts, each collapsed slightly under its own icing. Sofia had sold the rest before the school suspended rehearsals. Her trombone case still leaned against the filing cabinet because he had brought it to work for a repair appointment that no longer existed.

He was deciding whether stale doughnuts qualified as property when Jenn appeared in the doorway.

Deshpande stood behind her with Mara and a hospital recorder. Mel had seen the orientation footage. He knew not to look for a visible difference. That made him look harder.

Jenn said, “We need to ask you something, and the answer can be no.”

“That sentence has never improved a morning.”

“No.”

She asked permission to enter. He gave it. Deshpande remained in the hall until Mel separately invited him. The recorder announced the time.

Jenn placed a one-page form on the desk. “We have evidence that a meaningful exposure can begin a staged neurological progression over days. We have evidence that after progression, connected people can share perceptions, memories, and learned patterns. Ordinary distance, telecommunications outages, and electromagnetic shielding have not interrupted it. Deep sedation reduced one person’s contribution but did not erase information already received.”

Mel looked from her to Deshpande. Neither rushed him.

“And Nia?” he asked.

“Connected knowledge helped us recognize the risk,” Jenn said. “Clinicians examined her and treated her. The connection did not cure her.”

“Can she move her hand?”

“Some movement has returned. The outcome is not yet known.”

Jenn went through the rest without euphemism. The delayed prodrome. Source confusion. Severe distress during convergence. Loss of private mental boundaries. Unknown reversibility. Unknown long-term consequence. The possibility that desire and judgment would change before any later consent could be trusted as unchanged.

“What are you asking me to do?” Mel said.

“Accept a controlled exposure by eating prepared food. The preparation and amount will not be shown or described. You may refuse, wait, leave, or ask for another person. None of those choices changes your employment or Sofia’s access to care.”

Mel’s eyes went to the box beneath his hand.

“You picked doughnuts.”

“You brought them for Sofia’s marching-band fundraiser,” Jenn said. “You were holding the box when you first realized I might know what you were thinking.”

“You knew I was panicking.”

“I saw you brace the box against your chest and heard you ask whether I could hear you. I did not read an unconnected mind. You told me with your body and your question.”

Mel looked at the trombone case.

“If I say yes, does that put Sofia in?”

“No. Your assent applies only to you. We will record a no-contact requirement for Sofia and no exposure without her own lawful, active assent and appropriate safeguards.”

“Can you promise nobody changes that later?”

Jenn did not look at Deshpande before answering. “No. I can promise what we will record and follow now. I cannot truthfully bind every future connected person or institution.”

Mara slid a second sheet across the desk. At the top, in block capitals, it read: **SOFIA — NO CONTACT / NO DERIVATIVE ASSENT.**

Mel read every line. He asked for the waiting period. They gave it to him. He asked them to leave the office, and they left.

For forty minutes, he sat with the closed box and Sofia’s trombone case. Twice he reached for his phone. Once he dialed her number and hung up before the call connected. The no-contact sheet remained where Mara had placed it.

When he opened the door, all four of them were still in the corridor.

“I have questions,” he said.

Jenn nodded. “Ask them before you answer.”

They returned after lunch.

The fundraiser box was gone. In its place lay one clear specimen bag with a sealed label. Inside was a single glazed doughnut. The record identified it only as the vehicle for the disclosed exposure. Nothing on the label revealed preparation, amount, or method.

Mel sat behind his desk. Jenn and Deshpande sat opposite him. Mara operated the recorder.

“What’s the best part?” Mel asked.

Jenn said, “People have reported reduced loneliness, access to skills they did not learn, and relief from being misunderstood. Those reports come from people whose preferences may have changed. They are still reports, but they are not independent endorsements from unchanged observers.”

“That sounded rehearsed.”

“It was prepared because the distinction matters.”

“What’s the worst part?”

Jenn was quiet for several seconds.

“The pain was severe. Before convergence, I knew parts of my judgment were changing and could not reliably separate the change from my reasons. After convergence, I remember the person who feared losing ownership of her mind. I cannot prove to you that remembering her is the same as preserving her.”

Mel looked at Deshpande. “Same answer?”

“Substantially,” Deshpande said. “My experience differed in detail. The uncertainty does not.”

“Do you think you died?”

Jenn said, “We do not know.”

No one filled the silence.

Mel could have left. The door stood open. His keys were in his pocket. Sofia’s no-contact order had been copied to the hospital counsel and the receiving authority. Mara had arranged an unexposed physician to take over if he said no. There was a clean path from the chair to the parking lot, and everybody in the room had agreed to let him use it.

He thought about Nia lifting the foam ball with two fingers that morning. He thought about Jenn on the floor beside the doughnut boxes, lying badly to keep a frightened man from becoming a specimen. He thought about the fact that neither memory proved this was wise.

“If I change my mind after,” he said, “that doesn’t tell you I was right now.”

“Correct,” Jenn said.

“If I regret it before, you stop helping me do it.”

“Yes.”

“If Sofia comes here?”

“She is kept outside the exposure area and told the truth appropriate to her age. Your choice does not become hers.”

Mel signed the assent line. Mara asked him to state the choice aloud.

“I understand that I can wait or refuse. I understand the progression may be irreversible and may change the person who later evaluates this choice. I choose the disclosed exposure for myself. Not for Sofia.”

Mara signed as witness.

Jenn pushed nothing toward him. The bag remained in the center of the desk, beyond everybody’s hands.

Mel reached for it, stopped, and looked once more at the trombone case leaning against the filing cabinet. A strip of blue tape on the handle bore Sofia’s name in his block printing. One corner had peeled loose.

He pressed the corner down with his thumb.

Then he opened the labeled bag and had one.


## Part Four: No One Is Sick

The receptionist erased **PATIENT** and wrote **VISITOR**.

It was the twenty-ninth morning after Jenn’s bite.

The whiteboard had been mounted beside the entrance of what used to be a physical-therapy clinic. Its old lettering remained visible beneath the new paint: GAIT LAB, HYDROTHERAPY, PLEASE RETURN WALKERS. Someone had added a red line across the floor with tape. On the far side were low lights, six recliners, two examination rooms, and a kitchen that smelled of burnt toast. On Sofia’s side were three waiting-room chairs and a dead ficus.

She stayed with the ficus.

Mel had asked her to wait while he updated a record. He would drive her home afterward. She had agreed to those two things and nothing else. He repeated the terms before they entered, then asked the receptionist to repeat them. The woman had done so without smiling like Sofia was brave or frightened or fourteen.

“You are not entering orientation,” the receptionist said. “You can leave at any time. Your father’s appointment does not change your record.”

“I know.”

“I’m required to say it where the camera can hear.”

“I know that too.”

The receptionist nodded. “All right.”

Beyond the red line, Mel sat with a clipboard on his knee. He looked healthy. He had gained back the weight he lost during progression, and the tremor was gone. That made every person who had known him before stare at his hands.

Sofia no longer did. She watched his mouth instead, trying to catch the moment before he answered a question. There was always a moment. It did not tell her what she wanted it to tell her.

In the nearest examination room, a woman named Denise sat with her sister. Denise was four days into an accidental exposure and had come voluntarily after two nights without sleep. A Joined clinician kept the door open because Denise had asked for it open. He explained each action before taking it.

“I’d like to check your pupils.”

“Why?”

“To compare them with your intake examination and look for a change that needs ordinary medical treatment.”

“Is it part of making me one of you?”

“No.”

“Are you lying?”

“No.”

He held the light down by his thigh and waited. Denise eventually leaned forward. He examined one eye, asked again, and examined the other.

The room had no loose bottles, cleaning supplies, or hard objects within reach of the recliner. A framed photograph from Denise’s kitchen sat on the sill. Every sensation she reported went onto a pad with a possible source beside it. When she said she could feel cold rain on her neck, the clinician asked whether anyone she knew was outdoors. He did not tell her the answer. Her sister called Denise’s husband in Spokane and learned he had been standing under a leaking gutter.

At ten twenty-eight, Denise refused the next orientation step.

“I don’t want to practice listening,” she said.

The clinician closed the protocol. “All right.”

“That’s it?”

“You asked us to stop.”

“You said it would hurt more if I left.”

“I said some progressing people report greater distress when they resist attention. We do not know what you will experience.”

Denise stood. Her sister helped her with her coat. The receptionist called a car, packed water and a paper sick bag, and wrote the emergency number on the outside. No one blocked the red line. No one followed them into the parking lot.

Sofia watched the door close.

“Does that happen a lot?” she asked.

The receptionist said, “People leave.”

“That wasn’t my question.”

“Enough that the cars know where to wait.”

Forty-three minutes later, Denise came back without her sister. She held the wall as she crossed the waiting room. Her face was blotched and furious.

“It’s like I left a hand in here,” she said. “I can still feel it. It’s just not attached.”

The clinician met her at the red line and did not touch her.

“Do you want medical help only, or do you want to resume orientation?”

Denise pressed her forehead against the wall. “Resume.”

“Say it once more when you’re seated. You may stop again.”

Sofia looked at Mel. He had heard. His expression contained concern, but concern had become one of the things he wore too evenly.

He finished the form and brought it to the receptionist. The top page carried Sofia’s name and the old notation in his block printing: **NO CONTACT / NO DERIVATIVE ASSENT.** He added the date and wrote it again beneath the first line.

“Fresh copy to every center,” he said.

“Already queued,” the receptionist said.

Sofia stepped back to make room without crossing the tape. Mel waited until she picked up her trombone case, then held the outer door for her.

Behind them, Denise asked to begin again.

---

The world map showed eleven thousand seven hundred and six completed convergences.

Beside it, the death column contained one zero.

Mara had built the map in a borrowed office above a tax preparer. The wall still displayed a poster of a smiling family pointing at a refund amount nobody in the room could have earned. Her government laptop had been disabled remotely. She worked from paper, public reports, voluntary examination records, and photographs sent by clinicians who believed either that she could help or that she needed to stop being afraid.

She had separated observation from inference with colored pins. Blue meant a documented progression. White meant a travel route supported by tickets or access records. Yellow meant synchrony reported by at least two witnesses. Red meant a death plausibly caused by S-78.

The red box remained unopened.

The absence should have reassured her. Instead it destroyed every emergency sentence before she finished speaking it.

On the encrypted call, a deputy public-health director listened while Mara described the staged neurological process, the nonlocal correlations, the inability of shielding to interrupt them, and the consent problem after Stage 2. His counsel listened beside him with the patient expression of someone waiting for a noun to become actionable.

“Are the fully progressed individuals febrile?” the director asked.

“No.”

“Neurologically impaired?”

“Not by standard bedside testing. Their coordination and recall are often improved.”

“Requesting treatment?”

“No.”

“Being held against their will?”

“They do not describe themselves as separate captives.”

“That is not the same question.”

“It is why the question fails.”

Counsel spoke for the first time. “You’re asking for emergency quarantine authority over healthy people because they exchange information by a mechanism you cannot demonstrate with available instruments.”

“I can demonstrate the exchange.”

“Can you demonstrate harm?”

Mara looked at the zero.

“I can demonstrate a permanent loss of private cognitive boundaries and a progression that changes how later preference is formed.”

“Can you demonstrate that a fully progressed person wants those boundaries restored?”

“No.”

“Can you demonstrate restoration is possible?”

“No.”

The director rubbed his face. “Then I can offer voluntary examination, local clinical monitoring, and a legal review. I cannot detain eleven thousand people for excessive cooperation.”

“They’re still spreading.”

“With deaths?”

“Zero so far.”

“Forced exposures?”

“None documented during the current period.”

He did not reject the evidence. That would have been easier. He accepted each fact and declined the conclusion.

When the call ended, Mara wrote the decision beneath the map. Then she took out the contact sheet she had carried through three temporary offices.

Tom’s number was first.

She had called him twice after she disappeared. The first time she told him she was safe and could not explain. The second time he asked whether *safe* meant married, and she answered the question about her location instead. Since then, investigators had visited their house. Every call now created a route from him to her.

Mara crossed out his number.

She did not call to tell him why.

Arlo’s handwriting arrived in three countries before Mara did.

The first copied page came from a clinic in Winnipeg. The second was embedded in a Portuguese orientation checklist. The third appeared in a photograph of a laminated card taped beside a recliner in Osaka.

Each carried the same plain instruction:

**CLEANING SUPPLIES OUTSIDE REACH.**

Mara spread the pages across the tax preparer’s conference table. Beneath that line were later additions in different professional dialects: remove hard edges, identify a familiar anchor, verify borrowed facts, record the source when found, lower sensory load, do not interpret relief as renewed consent.
