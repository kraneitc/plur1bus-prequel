# Part Three: Have One

At 7:12 the next morning, Jenn and Arlo began doing each other’s jobs.

They were forty feet apart, behind two closed doors, with a nurses’ station and a supply alcove between them. Deshpande stood where he could see both rooms through separate panes of glass. Each room contained the same objects arranged in a different order: washbasin, folding chair, rolling tray, paper cup, box of tissues, plastic comb, legal pad, pen.

Jenn faced the wall in Room A. Arlo faced the wall in Room B.

“Blue chair,” Arlo said.

His chair was yellow.

“Tissues under the tray,” Jenn said.

Her tissues were on the sill.

Nia Santos, the night laboratory coordinator, wrote both answers on a form and checked the time against the wall clock. She had taped a second clock directly beneath it because Deshpande no longer trusted clocks that had not been made to disagree in public.

Arlo said, “Comb has two teeth missing.”

Jenn said, “One.”

Nia looked through the glass. The comb in Arlo’s room had one missing tooth. Jenn’s had two.

“Stop,” Deshpande said.

Nia lowered her pen. Jenn and Arlo turned around at the same time, then each noticed the other had done it and made a visible effort not to react.

Deshpande went into Jenn’s room. Mara Havel followed with the two packets of observations Jenn had delayed overnight. The pages had already been copied, numbered, and sealed in clear evidence sleeves. Jenn had written on scrap paper, medication wrappers, the cardboard back of a glove box, and once on the paper liner from an instrument tray. Her handwriting deteriorated near dawn but never became illegible.

Deshpande placed the packet on the rolling tray.

“Tell me why you withheld this.”

“I didn’t know if the correlation was real.”

“You knew it was worth recording.”

“Yes.”

“You knew the reporting rule.”

“Yes.”

“Then that answer is incomplete.”

Jenn rubbed the adhesive square on her forearm where an electrode had been removed. “I wanted one thing that happened before everybody started helping it happen.”

Deshpande waited.

“And I didn’t want Arlo turned into a container before we knew if he was sick.”

Through the glass, Arlo sat with his palms flat on his knees. A technician in his room asked him to identify three cards. He stared at the backs and named two correctly.

Deshpande said, “By withholding the observation, you increased the chance that neither of you would be protected.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Jenn looked at the sleeved pages. “I know that now.”

The distinction irritated him because it was accurate.

Mara held out an independent timeline. Arlo’s voice recorder had captured his first report of a sensation at 1:41. Jenn’s handwritten note describing the same sensation was timestamped at 1:40 by a hallway camera. Neither had been in sight of the other. A second pair aligned within eleven seconds. A third did not align at all.

The disorder in the record made fraud less likely. It also made the record useful.

The federal transfer order sat open on Deshpande’s tablet. Two secured vehicles were already waiting at the south loading bay. The receiving unit had better imaging, a larger isolation suite, and enough authority to make every person in the building stop improvising. It would also separate Jenn and Arlo by more than nine hundred miles before anyone had measured what separation changed.

“We comply with the transfer order for Arlo,” Deshpande said. “Not this morning. We request a twenty-four-hour hold for stabilization and paired observation.”

Mara looked at him. “They may deny it.”

“Then they deny a documented request.”

“And Jenn?”

“Remains here.”

Jenn said, “You’re keeping the study local.”

“I’m keeping the patients where the only verified observation can be repeated.”

“That’s a nicer sentence.”

“It is also a complete one.”

He signed the delay request. Beneath his name, in the field marked BASIS, he wrote: **Cooperation between exposed subjects may alter the phenomenon being measured.**

It was the first time he used *cooperation* in an official record. The word looked less scientific than *correlation* and more dangerous because it assigned a choice.

---

At 9:05, Jenn tasted toothpaste.

She was not brushing her teeth. She sat at the end of an examination table with her mouth closed while Arlo, in the other room, rinsed into a basin.

“Mint,” she said. “The aggressive kind. It hurts under the tongue.”

Arlo wiped his mouth. “That’s it.”

Nia checked the tube. “Spearmint.”

Jenn pointed at her tongue. “The package is lying.”

Nia almost smiled. Deshpande marked the report as sensory and drew a line beneath it. They had begun with four categories on the whiteboard: sensory, autonomic, mnemonic, semantic. Every report had to enter one of them or remain outside the grid. Deshpande preferred the reports outside. The empty margins still belonged to ignorance.

He changed only one condition at a time. Arlo closed his eyes. Jenn kept hers open. Arlo listened to a tone. Jenn wore hearing protection. Jenn counted backward by sevens. Arlo named tools from photographs. When either subject guessed, the guess remained in the record.

At 10:17, Arlo put both feet under his chair and said, “The red line.”

Jenn’s pencil stopped.

There had been a red line painted across the animal-room floor. On her first day in the building, years earlier, she had stepped over it without changing shoe covers. Arlo had corrected her in front of six people. He had kept his voice low and pointed to the posted procedure. She had laughed too loudly, called the rule theater, and walked back across the line while everyone waited.

She had not thought of it in months.

“Describe what you have,” Deshpande said to Arlo.

Arlo’s face had gone red. “Her stomach dropped. She wanted me to stop talking. Then she wanted me to say it louder so she could hate me instead of herself.”

Jenn stared at him through the glass.

“Is that accurate?” Deshpande asked.

“It’s rude.”

“That was not the question.”

“Yes.”

Arlo leaned forward. “I didn’t go looking for that.”

“Did you experience the event as a story?”

“No. It was just there. Red floor. Her shoe. That feeling.”

“Did you know whose memory it was before you saw her reaction?”

“Yes.”

Deshpande entered it under mnemonic, then added a second notation: emotional context preserved.

Jenn had crossed her arms. After a minute she let them fall.

“What?” Arlo asked.

“Nothing.”

“That’s not nothing.”

She looked at Nia instead of him. “I spent three years remembering him as pleased with himself.”

Arlo said, “I was terrified you’d touch the cages.”

“I know that now.”

Again, the distinction was exact. This time it did not irritate Deshpande.

For fourteen minutes the reported traffic weakened. When Jenn resumed the counting task and Arlo returned to the picture cards, it strengthened. Relief appeared to be as biologically relevant as attention. Deshpande wrote that in the margin, then crossed out *relief* and replaced it with *reduced defensive load*.

Jenn read it upside down.

“You made feeling better sound like a parking restriction.”

“It is currently a variable.”

“That must be comforting.”

“No.”

---

The photograph belonged to Arlo and had never left his phone.

He placed the phone face down on the tray in Room B. It was locked, in airplane mode, inside a clear evidence bag. Nia recorded the device identifiers and watched him enter the passcode under a folded privacy shield before locking it again. The screen went black.

“Choose an image you have not sent to anyone,” Deshpande said. “Do not choose the most memorable image on the device. Choose one you can locate again.”

Arlo nodded.

Jenn sat in Room A with a blank sheet of paper. “This is a terrible magic trick.”

“It is not a trick,” Deshpande said.

“That’s what makes it terrible.”

Arlo closed his eyes.

For the first minute, Jenn wrote nothing. Her fingers pressed hard enough into the pencil to whiten the nails. Then she said, “A woman at a kitchen counter. Green bag. Limes.”

Arlo opened his eyes.

“Continue,” Deshpande said.

“The top of the picture is blocked. Thumb, maybe. There’s a yellow dish towel over the oven handle. She’s laughing at whoever took it.” Jenn swallowed. “No. She’s laughing because he dropped one.”

Arlo sat very still.

Nia carried the sealed phone into the corridor, opened the bag on camera, and handed it to him. Arlo unlocked it and navigated without showing Jenn the screen. He stopped at an image and turned the phone toward the observation window.

His mother stood at a kitchen counter holding a green produce bag. Three limes rested beside the sink. A thumb obscured the upper corner. A yellow towel hung from the oven. Her mouth was open in laughter.

The device had not been accessed remotely. No file had moved. No network connection had been available. The information had arrived through Arlo’s memory of looking at the image.

Deshpande ordered the device logs preserved anyway.

Jenn pushed the pencil away. “Before anybody calls that a success, write down the rest.”

Nia looked to Deshpande. He nodded.

Jenn dictated slowly. “I was exposed. Arlo was exposed after material from me reached an existing break in his skin. Neither of us intended that. Symptoms developed over time. We delayed reporting. Directing attention at the effect may strengthen it. Strong emotion may strengthen or suppress it. We do not know whether practice changes the progression or only our ability to notice it.”

She looked through the glass at Arlo.

“And being able to show it off makes you want to show it off again.”

Arlo turned the phone face down.

Deshpande said, “Record that as a reported change in motivation.”

“Not a change,” Jenn said. “A pressure. I still know it’s a bad idea.”

“Can you separate the wish to cooperate from the wish to be believed?”

“No.”

“Can you separate either from the exposure?”

“No.”

He had Nia add both answers. Then he stopped the session while everyone still wanted another test.

---

The cup was red plastic with a white adhesive label around its middle.

Deshpande wrote his initials on the label, the date, and the time. Mara watched him cap the marker.

“You can still transfer them,” she said.

“Yes.”

“You can remain the unexposed principal observer.”

“Yes.”

“Those are not ceremonial questions.”

“I know.”

They were in the procedure room with the door open. Nia operated the camera. A physician from another service had reviewed Deshpande’s baseline examination and now stood beside a locked emergency cart, arms folded. Jenn sat beyond the marked boundary. Arlo watched from a video screen connected by a hard line.

Nothing about the material in the cup was shown or described. The record stated only that it was a known meaningful exposure by ingestion, prepared under institutional controls. No amount, preparation, or handling sequence appeared on the consent page.

Deshpande read the page aloud.

“Observed progression can include a delayed prodrome, altered sensory experience, access to perceptions or memories originating in other exposed people, impaired confidence about the source of one’s own thoughts, a period of severe neurologic and autonomic distress, and a lasting change in individual mental privacy and agency. The condition may be irreversible. Existing evidence is insufficient to establish treatment, immunity, long-term survival, or whether subjective continuity after full progression is equivalent to survival of the prior person.”

The language had taken three people forty minutes. It took him twenty-nine seconds to read.

Jenn said, “Add that judgment may change before the person reaches the point where everybody else can see it changing.”

Deshpande wrote the sentence in longhand and initialed it.

Mara said, “Why are you doing this?”

He had prepared an answer about longitudinal evidence. It was correct and inadequate.

“Because Jenn’s first reliable observations were contaminated by secrecy, and Arlo’s were contaminated by our response. A recorded voluntary exposure creates a baseline we do not have.”

“That is the study answer.”

“Yes.”

“I asked you.”

Deshpande looked at Jenn. She was rubbing the edge of the adhesive on her arm again, though it was no longer there.

“I required her to report an experience for which I had given her no language,” he said. “Then I treated the delay as if authority had remained intact. I want a better record.”

Mara waited.

He said, “And I will not ask her to cross the next interval while I stand outside it and call that objectivity.”

Jenn’s expression did not soften. “That second reason is about you.”

“Yes.”

“It might feel less noble later.”

“Yes.”

“You may become better at explaining why you wanted it.”

“Yes.”

The physician asked Deshpande to state whether he understood that he could decline without professional penalty, leave the room, remain unexposed, and continue directing the transfer and study.

“I understand.”

“Do you actively assent to the exposure described?”

“I do.”

Mara signed as witness. Nia signed as recorder. Jenn did not sign anything.

Deshpande lifted the marked cup. The contents had no notable smell. He drank once, set it down, and started the timer.

For the first minute, nothing happened.

This pleased him more than it should have.

---

On the first day, the timer became the most disliked person in the building.

It sounded every seventy-eight seconds during testing. Nia had selected a tone unlike any alarm used by the hospital, then discovered it was identical to the sound made by the break-room microwave when someone cancelled it with one second remaining. By noon, no one could heat soup without making Jenn look toward Arlo’s room.

The procedure was simple enough to survive fatigue. Establish baseline. Change one condition. Stop if distress rose or reporting became uncertain. Return to baseline. Record failures with the same care as successes.

At 11:14, Arlo looked at a photograph of a ladder. Jenn reported nothing.

At 11:16, he studied the photograph and silently named each rung. Jenn said, “Metal. Cold hand.”

At 11:19, he studied it while Nia played overlapping speech through headphones. Jenn reported a headache and no image.

At 11:22, they stopped.

The failures made the pattern legible. Directed attention strengthened it. Competing sensory load weakened it. Emotion sometimes broke through both and sometimes closed the route entirely. No one could summon arbitrary information. No one could reach anything Arlo did not know or perceive.

Deshpande developed a low fever by late afternoon. He marked the symptom on his own chart and continued until the independent physician ordered him to bed.

“I can sit in bed,” Deshpande said.

“That was not the ambiguity in my instruction.”

Jenn, overhearing from the next room, laughed for the first time that day.

On the second morning, Deshpande woke before the blood-pressure cuff inflated. He knew Nia had entered the room though the privacy curtain remained closed. What he had was not an image of her. It was the practiced caution of someone balancing two coffees in a cardboard carrier, one lid not fully seated.

“Stop,” he said.

The curtain moved. Nia froze behind it.

“Set the carrier down.”

There was a small pause, then the soft contact of cardboard against laminate.

Nia opened the curtain. “How did you know?”

Deshpande did not answer until she had marked the time.

“I knew what your hands were doing,” he said. “I did not know they were yours until you stopped.”

Jenn, seated in the doorway under supervision, watched him write the distinction.

“Welcome to the bad filing system,” she said.

---

They built three expensive rooms to demonstrate that the expensive rooms did nothing.

The first was an electromagnetic isolation chamber borrowed from imaging research. The second used active radio-frequency interference under engineering supervision. The third was a shielded clinical scanner that turned every loose coin into a meeting.

In each case, one exposed subject entered while another remained outside. Personal electronics were removed. Facility telecommunications were disabled for the interval. Timing came from isolated clocks compared before and after.

Jenn reported Arlo’s hand pain through the first chamber.

Arlo identified Jenn’s sudden nausea through the second.

During the scanner interval, Deshpande received the shape of a question Jenn had not yet spoken: whether the machine’s noise counted as sensory load or merely punishment.

The measured timing did not change beyond their established variation.

By then the federal hold had expired. Arlo was transferred under guard to a hospital across the state line. Deshpande protested the timing, documented the protest, and then used the transfer because refusing it would have turned observation into obstruction.

At 4:00 p.m., Arlo sat in a secured room hundreds of miles away and immersed his right hand in cold water for a supervised clinical test. At the local site, Jenn described the ache before the video feed arrived. Deshpande, who had not been told which hand, gripped his own right wrist.

Ordinary distance changed nothing they could measure.

When the telecommunications link was cut for the next interval, the reports continued. When the link returned, the two handwritten logs matched at three of the five planned markers and diverged at two. The rate was no better and no worse than when the subjects had been in adjacent rooms.

Mara stood beneath the whiteboard where Deshpande had written possible mechanisms. Every electromagnetic model had acquired enough red objections to resemble a failed municipal budget.

“No central source,” she said.

“No detected central source,” Deshpande corrected.

“No distance effect.”

“Within the distances tested.”

“No shielding effect.”

“Within the shielding tested.”

Mara capped the marker. “You know what sentence remains.”

Deshpande looked through the glass at Jenn. She was reading a consent form while, in another state, Arlo corrected a nurse on the location of a supply cabinet he had never visited. The cabinet location belonged to a progressing respiratory therapist working one floor below him. They traced it within six minutes.

Deshpande wrote beneath the discarded mechanisms: **The brain remains the processor and participates in a shared state not interrupted by ordinary distance or electromagnetic isolation.**

He disliked every word after *participates*.

Mara placed a thin folder on the table.

“Then you need this before you write the next sentence.”

---

The graph in Mara’s folder had two lines.

In an observed laboratory system, the untreated line climbed. The treated line flattened. Neither returned to zero.

“The antisense candidate appears to suppress new manufacture of the S-Seventy-Eight-associated complex,” Mara said. “It does not remove complexes already established.”

No target design appeared on the page. No sequence, amount, formulation, or route appeared anywhere in the folder. The page described an observed limit, not a recipe.

Deshpande turned it sideways as if the axes might confess to exaggeration.

“Confidence?”

“Enough to repeat. Not enough to call it treatment.”

“Human evidence?”

“None.”

“Toxicity?”

“Unknown in this context.”

“Delivery to the relevant cells?”

“Unestablished.”

Jenn stood at the far end of the table, where she could see the graph but not touch the folder. “Could it stop somebody before this starts?”

Mara said, “Possibly. That is not the same as reversing it.”

“Could it stop me?”

“The complexes are already established in you.”

“Arlo?”

“Same answer.”

Jenn looked at Deshpande.

He had developed smell loss that morning and his first source-less motor impression before lunch. Whatever uncertain window the graph represented, he was no longer outside the observed process.

Mara said, “I can request compassionate authorization. That is not a recommendation. It would be an uncontrolled human use with no established delivery and no evidence that suppressing new manufacture at your stage changes the outcome.”

“It could also destroy the only continuous observation,” Deshpande said.

Jenn shook her head. “Don’t hide behind the study.”

He looked at the two lines again. One climbed. One stopped climbing. Neither went backward.

“I decline an unvalidated intervention,” he said. “Record that I may now be subject to altered motivation and that preservation of the study may be one expression of it.”

Mara’s jaw tightened. “Also record that declining is medically defensible.”

“Yes.”

“And that neither choice is evidence of what you will want later.”

“Yes.”

She took the folder back. On the whiteboard, Deshpande printed in letters large enough to survive a bad photograph:

**PREVENTION IS NOT REVERSAL.**

Below it he added:

**OBSERVED SUPPRESSION IS NOT A CURE.**

---

Three days after Deshpande lifted the marked cup, Arlo’s scan failed because he could not stop moving.

The movement was not dramatic. His left foot flexed every few seconds, and a fine tremor traveled from his shoulder to his fingers whenever the scanner reached its loudest interval. Two attempts produced images blurred beyond clinical use. The receiving physician needed the scan to distinguish progression from a separate acute injury. Arlo could not safely remain in the machine for a third attempt without help.

The anesthesia team proposed a short period of monitored deep sedation.

Deshpande rejected the first plan because it included no independent neurological observations. The anesthesiologist rejected Deshpande’s revision because it treated a patient like a radio. They argued over a video link for nine minutes and produced a better plan than either had brought in.

Arlo listened from his bed.

“Do I get a vote?” he asked.

The anesthesiologist turned the camera toward herself. “Yes. The scan is clinically indicated. Sedation has its own risks. The network observation is incidental and will not change the anesthetic plan.”

“Can they watch what happens to it?”

“Only if that doesn’t interfere with your care.”

Arlo looked toward the ceiling. “Fine. Watch it fail another test.”

At the local site, Jenn, Deshpande, and Nia sat at separate desks with identical forms. They were not told when sedation began. Each marked the strength of any perception not originating in the room, once per minute, and described its character without trying to summon Arlo.

For six minutes, Deshpande had the ache in Arlo’s left shoulder and the irritating awareness of a loose thread under his gown. Jenn reported the scanner table under her spine. Nia, unexposed, reported nothing and controlled the clocks.

Then the impressions thinned.

They did not travel away. There was no sensation of distance and no door closing. Details simply stopped arriving. Deshpande retained facts he had already received, but nothing new joined them. Jenn wrote **quiet** three times, each on a different line.

During emergence, the first return was not a voice. Deshpande’s tongue felt too large for his mouth. Jenn reached for an emesis basin a second before Arlo gagged in the remote recovery room. A minute later they both reported the same confusion about why the ceiling tiles had moved.

The anesthesiologist’s record placed the quiet interval inside the period of deepest sedation. It did not align with a telecommunications change, a medication pump alarm, or any action at the local site.

Deshpande added the result to the model:

**Contribution depends on active brain function. Previously received information persists locally.**

“So sleep turns it off,” Nia said.

“We did not test ordinary sleep.”

“Being very unconscious turns it down.”

“In one subject, under one necessary clinical procedure.”

Jenn pressed a hand to her forehead. “He means yes, with enough footnotes to build a small bridge.”

Deshpande looked at the remote recovery feed. Arlo was awake enough to complain about the taste in his mouth. The complaint reached Jenn before the audio did.

“Not off,” Deshpande said. “Down.”

---

Nia dropped the clipboard at 2:26 in the afternoon.

It struck the floor edge-first, bounced against her shoe, and scattered three consent forms under the whiteboard. She swore, bent to collect them, and dropped the top page again.

Deshpande was reviewing his own six-hour cognitive scores. He looked up because his left hand had gone numb.

His hand was not numb.

Nia flexed hers twice. “Pins and needles.”

“Sit down,” Deshpande said.

“I slept on it at the desk.”

“Sit.”

She sat. Her speech was clear. Her face was symmetric. When he asked her to raise both arms, both rose. Her left fingers sagged before the arm did.

Deshpande knew the ordinary differential. Alongside it came something that did not belong to him: the memory of a veterinary neurologist demonstrating an apparently minor paw-placement error in an old sheepdog. The dog had walked normally until asked to place one paw on the edge of a stair. The lesion had not been in the paw.

The memory carried no name, only certainty about the danger of isolated distal weakness.

“Activate the clinical emergency team,” Deshpande said.

Nia shook her head. “It’s my hand.”

“Yes.”

“That is not an emergency team amount of body.”

“It can be.”

The local team arrived in two minutes. They repeated the examination, added tests Deshpande had not performed, and moved Nia for urgent treatment. He did not direct their care. He gave them the onset time and the observed change, then got out of the doorway.

For twenty-three minutes, he knew nothing.

Then the report came back: a small acute cerebral event, recognized early. Treatment had begun inside the useful window. Nia was awake, angry about being unable to finish the paperwork, and expected to recover much of the lost function.

The source of Deshpande’s borrowed pattern took longer to trace.

At 3:18, a progressing veterinary pathologist at a hospital in Córdoba recorded that she had been reviewing a canine neurology consultation when she experienced a sharp concern for a woman’s left hand. She had never met Deshpande or Nia. Her access to the old case came from her own training and the record open in front of her. The connection supplied experience, not omniscience. Local clinicians still had to examine Nia, identify the event, and act.

The result traveled through the building faster than any caution Deshpande attached to it.

By dinner, people were saying the patients had diagnosed a stroke through a wall.

By evening, someone said the patients had cured one.

Deshpande dictated a correction for the staff briefing: “Pooled learned pattern recognition prompted timely ordinary medical evaluation. It did not replace that evaluation and did not determine the outcome.”

Jenn listened from her bed.

“Nobody is going to remember that sentence,” she said.

“Then we will repeat it.”

“They’ll remember Nia can use her hand.”

From the corridor came the sound of applause as Nia was wheeled past on her return from imaging. She lifted her right hand because the left was wrapped around a foam ball.

Deshpande watched Jenn watch the doorway.

The pressure in the room was no longer only to demonstrate the phenomenon. It was to deserve the applause.

---

Jenn taped her home address to the ceiling before the shaking started.

She had written it in block capitals on a sheet of copier paper. Below it she added the names of three people permitted in the room, the word **JENN**, and a sentence Deshpande had required from every baseline interview:

**A thought can feel like mine without beginning in me.**

At 11:08 that night, she looked up from a tray of untouched food and said, “Timer.”

Nia was still under observation elsewhere, so Mara started it. Deshpande took the chair beside the bed. His own symptoms had advanced: intermittent tremor, smell loss, source confusion under fatigue. He remained able to follow the protocol and had an independent physician in the room to remove him if that changed.

Jenn gripped the bed rail.

Her first convulsion lifted one shoulder and struck it against the mattress. The second traveled through her legs. Deshpande moved the tray, lowered the rail only when the nurse told him to, and kept his hands away from hers until she reached.

She reached on the fourth spasm.

“Address,” she said.

He read it from the ceiling.

“Again.”

He read it again.

At six minutes she vomited into the basin. At nine she cried without making a sound. At eleven she said the address with him and lost the street number halfway through. The monitors showed strain without a correctable mechanical cause. The staff treated what they could treat: airway, position, hydration, injury prevention. Nobody pretended those measures stopped the progression.

Deshpande experienced fragments that might have been Jenn’s and might have been his response to watching her. The taste of toothpaste. The animal-room floor. Mel’s daughter blowing one sour note on a trombone. He marked none of them. During convergence, observation could become intrusion merely by being written down.

“Arlo?” Jenn asked at fourteen minutes.

“Awake. Stable. Remote.”

“Can he feel this?”

“He reports distress and motor tension. He is not convulsing.”

“Good.”

The next wave folded her around the basin. When it passed, she said, “That was a lie.”

“What was?”

“Good.”

Deshpande waited.

“I want him here,” she said. “I don’t want him to have this. Both are true.”

“Both are recorded.”

At nineteen minutes she began repeating her name. At twenty-two, she alternated it with Arlo’s. At twenty-five, she stopped recognizing which person had first learned the home address. Deshpande pointed to the ceiling, and she read each line aloud as if administering it to someone else.

At thirty-one minutes the shaking eased. Her pulse did not. She stared at Deshpande and said, “You’re frightened of enjoying the relief.”

He had not told her that.

“Is that your inference?” he asked.

“It’s here.”

“Whose fear is it?”

Jenn closed her eyes. “Yes.”

The answer was not humorous. It was the only one she had.

At thirty-six minutes she asked for the address again. At thirty-eight, she read it without moving her eyes to the page. At thirty-nine, her hand released his.

At forty minutes, the tremor ended.

Jenn lay still. Her breathing slowed. The nurse checked her pupils and asked her name.

“Jennifer Lafferty,” she said.

“Where are you?”

She named the building and room.

“Do you know what happened?”

Jenn looked at Deshpande. Her face was exhausted, wet, and familiar. Nothing visible announced a border had disappeared.

“Yes,” she said. “You can take the address down now.”

From that answer forward, Deshpande observed her as he would any other fully Joined person: by speech, behavior, physiology, and what could be independently traced. Whatever private viewpoint Jenn had possessed no longer belonged to the record.

---

They conducted the orientation at 12:03 a.m.

Jenn sat upright with two blankets around her shoulders. The paper bearing her address remained taped to the ceiling because nobody had yet found a ladder. Mara read from the protocol Deshpande had drafted before his own exposure.

“Do you understand that you experienced a progressive neurological event?”

“Yes.”

“Are you in pain?”

“Less than ten minutes ago. My jaw and back still hurt.”

“Do you have access to perceptions or memories originating in other connected people?”

“Yes.”

“All people?”

“No. Only connected people, and not everything they know. Access varies with attention, state, and the person.”

“Can you perceive the private thoughts of anyone unconnected?”

“No.”

“Can you knowingly give us a false answer?”

Jenn paused. “I cannot form the intention to make you believe something I know is false. I can be mistaken. I can lack information. I can refuse. I can answer narrowly unless the context requires more.”

Mara glanced at Deshpande, then continued.

“Do you believe the person who consented before exposure is continuous with you?”

“I remember being her. That does not establish the answer you want.”

“Do you want this condition reversed?”

“Part of what you mean by *want* depended on separation. I do not experience that separation now. I would not resist a safe reversal on behalf of the person who asked for one, but there is no demonstrated reversal.”

Deshpande leaned forward. “Can consent given before exposure remain unaltered after exposure?”

“No. Exposure can alter judgment before full convergence, and convergence alters the structure in which preference is experienced.”

“Then is my earlier consent still valid?”

“It establishes that you chose exposure while unexposed and informed. It cannot establish what an unchanged version of you would choose now, because no unchanged version is available for comparison.”

“Should I continue?”

Jenn’s hands rested open on the blankets. “You have a clear opportunity to withdraw from testing, report your altered state, and request clinical care. The antisense candidate is not a demonstrated reversal and has no established human use. Continuing may produce severe distress, permanent loss of mental privacy, and a change that makes later endorsement unreliable as evidence of present consent.”

“That is not an answer.”

“It is the material information. I do not know what you should choose.”

Mara closed the protocol. “We can stop the study now. Transfer the records. Treat what can be treated. You do not owe the experiment a conclusion.”

Deshpande knew the offer was real. He could feel Jenn’s attention on him, but not as coercion and not as permission. The door to the corridor stood open. His physician waited there with transport orders unsigned.

He took the last private-observation sheet from his folder and handed it to Mara.

“You keep this,” he said. “If I ask for it after convergence, you refuse until the independent review.”

“That is not the same as leaving.”

“No.”

“Are you choosing to proceed while you can still state the choice separately?”

“Yes.”

Mara accepted the sheet but did not forgive him for making the acceptance necessary.

---

Deshpande spent his last private hour correcting nouns.

He changed *signal* to *reported perception*. He changed *transmission* to *shared access*. He struck *sender* wherever it appeared without a named human source. The edits were small and vain. They were also his.

At 1:14, he began the final baseline interview with Mara. Jenn watched from the other side of the room. She did not prompt him.

“Name,” Mara said.

He gave it.

“Current place.”

He gave the building, floor, and room.

“State the reason you remained.”

“To preserve a continuous observation and accompany Jenn through a risk I asked her to document.”

“State the reason against remaining.”

“The observer becomes part of the process. My judgment may already be altered. The result may destroy the authority required to interpret it.”

At 1:23, his right leg began to shake.

He asked for the timer. Mara started it and read back his answers. The first convulsion pulled his shoulder toward his ear. He kept his pen on the page and recorded the time in numbers that narrowed toward the margin.

He knew when the room temperature changed because Jenn felt the air handler start. He knew Arlo was awake in another state because Arlo was annoyed by a blood-pressure cuff. He knew a child in Córdoba was drawing a horse at the foot of her mother’s hospital bed, though he did not know the child’s name and could not see the whole horse.

Every impression arrived with context enough to tempt him to call it understanding.

It was not understanding. It was access.

At nine minutes, Mara asked him to state his name. He did. At twelve, she asked him to distinguish a memory of Jenn’s from one of his. He chose the red line and got it wrong: he had observed the account, but the shame now carried the weight of recollection.

At sixteen minutes, he laughed.

Mara said, “What happened?”

“Nia is trying to complete a form with her right hand.”

“Can you verify that?”

“Not yet.”

“Then mark it unverified.”

He did. The correction steadied him.

At twenty-one minutes, the pain took language away. His muscles pulled against one another without coordination. The physician and nurse kept his body from the hard edges of the bed. Jenn answered questions about what had helped her and did not claim it would help him.

Mara read his baseline answers again.

The words sounded pompous and dear. He had believed ownership meant the right to control an account. He saw, in the narrowing space still available to him, that ownership had depended on a boundary: this error was his to make; this reason was his to conceal; this shame could be offered or withheld.

The boundary was not virtue. Its loss was not automatically virtue either.

At thirty-four minutes, Arlo’s irritation at the cuff became Jenn’s amusement, became a warmth in Deshpande’s chest, and returned to Arlo as embarrassment. The circulation of it was faster than speech and less precise than a sentence.

He placed the pen on the private-observation sheet Mara had returned only for the active interval. He wrote:

**I can still identify the wish to remain separate.**

At thirty-seven minutes, he added:

**I can no longer establish that the wish begins only here.**

Mara asked his name.

He answered correctly.

She asked whether he wanted the procedure stopped.

“There is no procedure to stop,” he said. “Support only.”

It was a correction. It was also an evasion. He opened his mouth to say so, and the distinction ceased to belong to one place.

His hand moved to the next line. He formed the first downward stroke of a capital **I**.

The pen stopped halfway.

The monitors continued. The timer continued. Mara recorded the endpoint by behavior and examination, without claiming access to anything private. Jenn stood when the nurse told her she could. Deshpande’s body lay quiet beneath the blanket.

When he spoke again, he answered Mara’s questions accurately. No one entered his viewpoint to decide what accuracy meant.

Four days after Jenn and Arlo’s first verified echo, Mel found the last fundraiser box under his desk.

Someone had put his coat over it. The coat now smelled faintly of glaze. He lifted the lid and counted eleven doughnuts, each collapsed slightly under its own icing. Sofia had sold the rest before the school suspended rehearsals. Her trombone case still leaned against the filing cabinet because he had brought it to work for a repair appointment that no longer existed.

He was deciding whether stale doughnuts qualified as property when Jenn appeared in the doorway.

Deshpande stood behind her with Mara and a hospital recorder. Mel had seen the orientation footage. He knew not to look for a visible difference. That made him look harder.

Jenn said, “We need to ask you something, and the answer can be no.”

“That sentence has never improved a morning.”

“No.”

She asked permission to enter. He gave it. Deshpande remained in the hall until Mel separately invited him. The recorder announced the time.

Jenn placed a one-page form on the desk. “We have evidence that a meaningful exposure can begin a staged neurological progression over days. We have evidence that after progression, connected people can share perceptions, memories, and learned patterns. Ordinary distance, telecommunications outages, and electromagnetic shielding have not interrupted it. Deep sedation reduced one person’s contribution but did not erase information already received.”

Mel looked from her to Deshpande. Neither rushed him.

“And Nia?” he asked.

“Connected knowledge helped us recognize the risk,” Jenn said. “Clinicians examined her and treated her. The connection did not cure her.”

“Can she move her hand?”

“Some movement has returned. The outcome is not yet known.”

Jenn went through the rest without euphemism. The delayed prodrome. Source confusion. Severe distress during convergence. Loss of private mental boundaries. Unknown reversibility. Unknown long-term consequence. The possibility that desire and judgment would change before any later consent could be trusted as unchanged.

“What are you asking me to do?” Mel said.

“Accept a controlled exposure by eating prepared food. The preparation and amount will not be shown or described. You may refuse, wait, leave, or ask for another person. None of those choices changes your employment or Sofia’s access to care.”

Mel’s eyes went to the box beneath his hand.

“You picked doughnuts.”

“You brought them for Sofia’s marching-band fundraiser,” Jenn said. “You were holding the box when you first realized I might know what you were thinking.”

“You knew I was panicking.”

“I saw you brace the box against your chest and heard you ask whether I could hear you. I did not read an unconnected mind. You told me with your body and your question.”

Mel looked at the trombone case.

“If I say yes, does that put Sofia in?”

“No. Your assent applies only to you. We will record a no-contact requirement for Sofia and no exposure without her own lawful, active assent and appropriate safeguards.”

“Can you promise nobody changes that later?”

Jenn did not look at Deshpande before answering. “No. I can promise what we will record and follow now. I cannot truthfully bind every future connected person or institution.”

Mara slid a second sheet across the desk. At the top, in block capitals, it read: **SOFIA — NO CONTACT / NO DERIVATIVE ASSENT.**

Mel read every line. He asked for the waiting period. They gave it to him. He asked them to leave the office, and they left.

For forty minutes, he sat with the closed box and Sofia’s trombone case. Twice he reached for his phone. Once he dialed her number and hung up before the call connected. The no-contact sheet remained where Mara had placed it.

When he opened the door, all four of them were still in the corridor.

“I have questions,” he said.

Jenn nodded. “Ask them before you answer.”

---

They returned after lunch.

The fundraiser box was gone. In its place lay one clear specimen bag with a sealed label. Inside was a single glazed doughnut. The record identified it only as the vehicle for the disclosed exposure. Nothing on the label revealed preparation, amount, or method.

Mel sat behind his desk. Jenn and Deshpande sat opposite him. Mara operated the recorder.

“What’s the best part?” Mel asked.

Jenn said, “People have reported reduced loneliness, access to skills they did not learn, and relief from being misunderstood. Those reports come from people whose preferences may have changed. They are still reports, but they are not independent endorsements from unchanged observers.”

“That sounded rehearsed.”

“It was prepared because the distinction matters.”

“What’s the worst part?”

Jenn was quiet for several seconds.

“The pain was severe. Before convergence, I knew parts of my judgment were changing and could not reliably separate the change from my reasons. After convergence, I remember the person who feared losing ownership of her mind. I cannot prove to you that remembering her is the same as preserving her.”

Mel looked at Deshpande. “Same answer?”

“Substantially,” Deshpande said. “My experience differed in detail. The uncertainty does not.”

“Do you think you died?”

Jenn said, “We do not know.”

No one filled the silence.

Mel could have left. The door stood open. His keys were in his pocket. Sofia’s no-contact order had been copied to the hospital counsel and the receiving authority. Mara had arranged an unexposed physician to take over if he said no. There was a clean path from the chair to the parking lot, and everybody in the room had agreed to let him use it.

He thought about Nia lifting the foam ball with two fingers that morning. He thought about Jenn on the floor beside the doughnut boxes, lying badly to keep a frightened man from becoming a specimen. He thought about the fact that neither memory proved this was wise.

“If I change my mind after,” he said, “that doesn’t tell you I was right now.”

“Correct,” Jenn said.

“If I regret it before, you stop helping me do it.”

“Yes.”

“If Sofia comes here?”

“She is kept outside the exposure area and told the truth appropriate to her age. Your choice does not become hers.”

Mel signed the assent line. Mara asked him to state the choice aloud.

“I understand that I can wait or refuse. I understand the progression may be irreversible and may change the person who later evaluates this choice. I choose the disclosed exposure for myself. Not for Sofia.”

Mara signed as witness.

Jenn pushed nothing toward him. The bag remained in the center of the desk, beyond everybody’s hands.

Mel reached for it, stopped, and looked once more at the trombone case leaning against the filing cabinet. A strip of blue tape on the handle bore Sofia’s name in his block printing. One corner had peeled loose.

He pressed the corner down with his thumb.

Then he opened the labeled bag and had one.
