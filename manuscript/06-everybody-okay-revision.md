# Part Six: Everybody Okay?

The pharmacist could not smell the peppermint oil.

The bottle had leaked inside a delivery carton and soaked one corner dark. Lena Cho noticed because the cardboard felt cold against her wrist. Three customers stood at the counter asking for water. A fourth had fallen asleep in a blood-pressure chair with his mouth open.

“Summer virus,” her technician said.

It was not summer.

Lena placed the damaged bottle in a sealed waste bag, washed the oil from her hand, and called the supplier. The representative said six other pharmacies had reported broken bottles that morning. He could smell every one of them. The damage was ordinary. Lena’s missing smell was not.

She wrote **NO ODOR** on a sticky note and put it beside the register. Then she drank from the tap until the technician asked whether she was all right.

Across the city, people continued working through symptoms that did not yet form a reason to stop.

A hotel clerk recognized a guest he had never seen and correctly remembered the man’s childhood dog. He blamed a social-media photograph he could not locate. Two sanitation workers argued over which of them had dreamed about a flooded basement in another country. An anesthetist paused before a scheduled case because she knew, without a remembered source, that the surgeon’s left shoe was untied beneath the drape.

Emergency departments grouped the thirst, poor sleep, smell disturbance, and brief confusion under a mild viral-syndrome code. No test found the expected virus. Most patients walked in, received water and instructions, and walked out.

Online, someone named it déjà flu. The phrase spread faster than the clinical notice because it required no billing category.

Daryl Kemp drove a school bus. He had slept three hours and kept tasting onions though he had eaten toast. At the depot, a Joined safety worker told every symptomatic driver to stop at the first tremor. Daryl asked whether the worker was ordering him off the route.

“I am telling you the known risk,” the worker said. “You are still legally permitted to drive.”

“That is a stupid system.”

“Yes.”

Daryl took the route because forty-two children still needed collecting and he did not feel sick. Before pulling out, he tore the back from a timetable and wrote in black marker:

**PULL OVER IF HANDS SHAKE.**

He taped it above the speedometer.

Thirty-six hours remained before the last major convergence wave ended.

The discarded olive jar sat in the bin beneath the prepared-food receipt.

Four days had passed since the delivery. Mara laid the receipt on the safe-house counter and wrote the symptoms beside the timestamp.

Day one: nothing she had recognized.

Day two: thirst, reduced smell, poor sleep.

Day three: the four-second tremor at the sample vault.

Day four: a memory of a grocery scanner that was not hers, and Tom’s certainty that she hated a green lid.

The memory did not arrive as a story. Fluorescent light shone on a black scanner bed. A hand with chipped silver nail polish turned the olive jar to find the code. Then came Tom’s fond, exasperated recognition of her substitution note. The two fragments carried different textures because they came from different nervous systems: the fulfillment worker’s current sight and Tom’s domestic memory.

Mara verified what she could. The fulfillment record named the employee. Public clinic data placed that employee among completed convergences. Tom’s orientation record fixed his endpoint eighty-one hours after exposure. The order log showed when the prepared meal entered the delivery chain. It did not show preparation, amount, or handling, and Mara did not try to reconstruct them.

The broad route was enough.

She had been exposed through prepared food four days earlier, after acceleration began.

Mara lifted her left hand. The wedding ring looked wrong for the width of one breath. It was her ring, bought in a store with a broken air conditioner, resized after Tom lost weight, scratched on a filing cabinet during their first move. At the same time it was the ring seen from across a hardware-store aisle by a man who had wondered why she was laughing at the wrong tap.

The interval ended. The ring belonged to her again, but ownership had become something she could observe changing.

Her stock ledger was at the original laboratory. The remaining antisense case sat in the locked slot under a label she had written before she knew she would need to read it.

The early window might already be closing. Nothing in the evidence promised otherwise. Established complexes could not be dismantled by destroying a freezer, a sample, a room, or a transmitter that did not exist.

Mara packed the receipt, symptom sheet, ring photograph, and baseline tests. She left the olive jar in the bin.

When she locked the safe-house door, she wrote the time on the back of her hand.

Operators removed their headsets when orders conflicted with facts they could not source.

Rios stopped ordering them to put the headsets back on.

The military command network had not been sabotaged. Its people still had power, radios, fuel, and functioning consoles. What failed was the interval between hearing an order and deciding that the order belonged to the world in front of them.

One operator abandoned an airfield closure because he could feel a medical transport low on fuel. The aircraft existed and the fuel warning was real, supplied by a progressing crew chief at another installation. Another operator opened a road Rios needed closed because connected paramedics were using it to reach a seizure cluster.

Rios changed her method.

“Keep the east corridor open for Ambulance Six,” she said. “Its purpose is access to County General. Close the west approach because a tanker is disabled across two lanes. Review in six minutes.”

The operator kept the headset on.

Orders whose physical purpose could be checked survived longer than orders based on authority alone. Rios reduced every instruction to a body, route, hazard, and review time. Some corridors held.

Her mouth had been dry since midnight. When she lifted her secure phone, the fingers of her right hand trembled against the case.

She put the phone down and pressed the hand flat on the desk.

Exhaustion. Too much coffee. Not enough coffee. The command post had eaten prepared food from three deliveries during the acceleration window, but no completed exposure reconstruction had reached her. A hypothesis was not a diagnosis, and she did not have time for either.

Major Yoon saw the hand. “Medical observation. Now.”

“No.”

“You wrote the Stage 3 containment guidance.”

“I wrote it for personnel whose removal did not collapse the command function.”

“That sentence is why it applies to you.”

Rios’s phone lit with an ordinary message from Leah: **Mateo brought in after collision. County General. Trauma two. Come now.**

No network supplied the location. No progressing aide read it for her. Leah had typed it with a shaking hand and sent it through a cellular system that happened to work.

Rios transferred corridor authority to Yoon, took the vehicle keys from a medically cleared driver, and left command before anyone could place her under observation.

On the road to County General, her secure phone displayed Mateo’s location until the battery warning covered his name.

Davis’s agriculture title was highlighted in yellow on the continuity list.

The list was intended for cabinet officials, constitutional successors, senior commanders, and people who could keep water, power, food, and communications functioning after an attack. Davis was an under secretary whose name appeared beneath a heading copied from a department organization chart. Someone had highlighted the office instead of checking the rank.

He showed the mistake to the boarding officer.

“The Secretary is on another transport,” Davis said.

“Agriculture is marked essential.”

“Agriculture is. I am not agriculture.”

The officer looked at the bus, the line behind Davis, and the instruction to load highlighted names. “Today you are.”

Davis boarded.

His portfolio did touch livestock laboratories, food distribution, rural communications, cold storage, emergency feeding, and enough contradictory continuity plans to make him useful in a room without the Secretary. That did not make the list correct. It made correction slower than departure.

He took an aisle seat and opened the agriculture briefing he had carried from the authorization meeting. In the margin he wrote:

**08:14 — thirst.**

At 08:37, the woman across the aisle began humming a hymn Davis remembered from a church he had never attended. He wrote the time.

At 09:02, he could not smell the driver’s coffee though he saw steam rising from it. He wrote the time.

At 09:18, his left foot knew the pressure of a brake pedal while both his feet rested flat on the bus floor. He wrote **motor impression / source unknown** and underlined *unknown*.

The people around him were senior enough to be unused to reporting symptoms to one another. Davis passed the briefing down the aisle and asked them to add times anyway. A deputy secretary wrote in a space reserved for livestock mortality. A judge used the margin beside rural broadband.

The bus carried water, emergency rations, and armed protection. It carried no suction equipment, seizure pads, neurological medication cart, or staff sized for a mass transition.

Davis told the boarding officer at the bunker entrance.

“It’s a continuity facility,” she said.

“A bunker is not a ward.”

“It is the place on the list.”

The reinforced door closed on the bus before anyone found another place.

The clocks disagreed by seven minutes.

The neural peaks did not.

At support sites across the city, unconnected wall clocks, battery timers, vehicle displays, and oven panels reached different numbers while monitored Stage 3 hosts showed the same narrow rise every seventy-eight seconds. The shift used no radio frequency and waited for no time zone. It was visible only because brains and bodies responded.

The Joined were not activating uninfected people. No shared-state change could build an interface in a nervous system that lacked one. Exposure had occurred days earlier through thousands of local events. The synchronized steps acted only on mature interfaces already manufactured by infected brains.

Mara felt the first global step while driving toward the laboratory.

Her shoulders lowered. A migraine she had been holding behind her eyes released by one degree. Through the opening came a paramedic’s memory of kneeling beside a blue bicycle and a child’s certainty that the moon followed their car. Neither belonged to the road in front of her.

She gripped the steering wheel and named them aloud.

“Paramedic memory. Child memory. Not mine.”

Seventy-eight seconds later, they carried emotional weight. The paramedic had failed to save the cyclist. The child had been delighted. Grief and delight occupied her chest without cancelling each other.

“Back,” Mara said.

The access thinned. The migraine returned harder.

On the road to County General, Rios heard Mateo laugh at the age of six. The memory came from Leah, who was in Stage 3 and looking at their injured son. Rios knew the source because Leah’s angle placed a trauma light behind his head. She struck the steering wheel once.

“Out.”

The memory withdrew enough for the lane markings to become singular again.

At schools, clinics, terminals, homes, and vehicles, progressing people made different requests.

“Closer.”

“Get out.”

“Please make it stop.”

“Don’t leave.”

The network reduced panic where it could and continued the steps where it could not. Supported cohorts were medically rational. Consent had already been removed from the calculation.

Daryl Kemp felt the first tremor in his right hand as the school bus entered a residential street. The warning above the speedometer blurred each time another person’s eyes supplied a different road.

He read his own block letters aloud.

“Pull over if hands shake.”

He turned on the hazard lights.

Tess Arnold’s shutdown checklist was taped beside the pencil log Rios had copied.

The tower crane moved a suspended load over a cleared section of the worksite. Wind remained within the site limit. Ground crew wore hard hats and watched her cab because the alert had told every operator to stop at the first tremor.

Tess’s right thumb jumped against the control.

She took her hand away.

“Shutting down,” she said over the site radio. “Clear the exclusion zone.”

The spotter repeated the order. Workers moved beyond the marked boundary. Tess checked the first box.

She lowered the load toward the nearest safe resting point. Checked the second.

She set the brake. Checked the third.

Her left arm filled with the sensation of holding a newborn against a hospital gown. She had never given birth. The memory came with a name she could not keep and a love so complete it made the crane cab disappear.

“Not mine,” Tess said.

The cab returned. The load was still moving.

She corrected the swing and notified the site that she was remaining in place. Fourth box. The last step was to secure power after the load settled.

The next shared-state step arrived.

Tess knew the exact location of every worker beneath her through connected eyes. The knowledge should have helped. It entered beside vertigo from a roof in Seoul, nausea from a ferry, and the motor intention of a thousand hands bracing against floors. Her own hands stopped being the loudest pair.

The load touched the resting point on one edge and swung free.

“Zone clear?” Tess asked.

“Clear,” the spotter answered.

She reached for the final control. Her fingers closed six inches to the left of it.

The suspended load struck the unfinished structure. Steel folded with a sound she felt through the tower before she heard it. The cab whipped sideways. Tess’s shoulder hit the window frame and something in it broke.

The load remained inside the exclusion zone. No worker stood beneath it.

Her checklist slid to the floor. Four boxes were checked. The fifth remained empty.

Tess tried to tell the ground crew that she was still conscious. What came out was a stranger’s address.

Joined responders began climbing toward the cab while she could still hear her name over the radio.

Two airport medical areas stood almost empty.

The third had run out of chairs.

Nurse Alina Ford moved between travelers on the floor, tagging airway risk, injury, seizure activity, and level of orientation. She was uninfected by every available test and felt none of the shared-state shifts the patients kept describing. Her ignorance made her useful. It also made every calm Joined volunteer look like a person pretending not to hear an alarm.

The two quieter areas served the terminals Omar had corrected. Their progressing clusters remained within the support capacity positioned there. The adjacent terminal held three times as many symptomatic travelers and twice the medical staff, neither number sufficient.

Omar stood beside the operations desk with both hands braced on a maintenance map. His speech was slow. Between questions, his gaze moved toward events beyond the room.

“This corridor stays clear,” he told Alina.

He pointed to a service route on the ordinary access map. The route identified doors wide enough for stretchers, a freight elevator, and a passage that avoided passenger stairs. It contained no exposure equipment, calculation, or plan.

“Can you walk it?” Alina asked.

“Yes.”

He took one step and his right knee folded.

Alina and a Joined baggage handler lowered him into a chair. Omar objected to the handler touching the map before he objected to being caught.

“Don’t change my markings.”

“We won’t,” the handler said.

“I wasn’t talking to all of you.”

“I know.”

Another seventy-eight-second peak passed through the monitored travelers. Omar’s head lifted. His fingers tapped the map in a pattern Alina had seen in three other Stage 4 patients moments before their language began to change.

“Name,” she said.

“Omar Haddad.”

“Where are you?”

“Terminal operations. Airport.”

“What did you change?”

“I stopped an excess in two terminals.”

“Who made the change?”

“I did.”

His shoulders shook. Alina moved the map out of his reach only after he nodded permission. The Joined handler repeated the safe access route to the incoming medical team, exactly as Omar had marked it.

At the next check, Omar said, “We need the freight elevator open.”

Alina wrote the time and drew a line beneath his prior use of **I**. She did not decide, from one pronoun, that convergence had completed. She kept examining him until orientation, source ownership, and behavior changed together.

Before his speech lost the singular entirely, she wrote **OMAR HADDAD — ORIGINAL CORRECTION SOURCE** at the top of the report.

Daryl pulled the bus to the curb.

He switched on the hazards. His left hand shook against the wheel while his right reached for the parking brake. A child in the third row began shouting that she could taste blood. The blood belonged to her connected father biting his tongue at a warehouse two miles away. Knowing the source did not stop her screaming.

Daryl’s right hand closed around the brake.

The next shared-state step brought another driver’s foot pressing a pedal on a road in rain. Daryl pushed where that foot expected resistance. His own foot slid off the service brake.

The bus began to roll.

He pulled the parking control halfway before his fingers opened. The bus crossed a driveway, struck a parked car, and stopped against a low brick wall. The impact threw two children from their seats. One broke a wrist. Another struck his head and did not answer for nineteen seconds.

The Joined safety worker following in a support van reached them before the hazards completed another cycle. He could stabilize a neck, call the right trauma service, and direct frightened children toward grass. He could not go backward and finish Daryl’s hand movement.

At County General, the anesthetist from the morning list felt her jaw begin to tremble during a procedure. She named it immediately, asked for her replacement, and completed the medication handoff before leaving the room. In the corridor, with the patient safe behind her, she collapsed beside the supply cart and aspirated before suction reached her.

On a residential ladder, a woman borrowed another person’s vertigo and reached for a handhold that existed in the other person’s kitchen. Her hand closed on air. She fell one story into a hedge, survived, and lay there reciting the name of a city she had never visited.

At the band room, the youngest clarinet player stiffened beside the concrete threshold. Sofia caught her under the arms and lowered her onto the nearest mat.

“Look at me,” Sofia said.

The child looked past her. “My mom is in a blue room.”

“Your mom is at work.”

“Blue room.”

Mel confirmed the source through a connected hospital worker: the child’s mother had been moved into a blue-curtained observation bay. The fact calmed the child for eight seconds. Then a tremor folded her knees.

Sofia stayed on the mat. Her own thirst had become painful. A sour brass taste filled her mouth although her trombone lay six feet away. When she looked at the folded no-contact paper, she remembered Mel writing it from the wrong side of the desk.

The memory was his.

“Get out of my head,” she said.

Mel remained beyond arm’s reach. “I cannot remove an established interface. I can reduce attention if you ask.”

“I am asking.”

The borrowed angle thinned. The effort of holding it away left her panting.

Around them, some transitions lasted long enough for people to narrate every lost boundary. Others arrived with seizure, aspiration, falls, or delirium. Joined responders caught bodies, protected airways, moved vehicles, and still ran out of hands.

The band director set the metronome to seventy-eight seconds because he wanted one stable interval the children could see. He stopped conducting when his arms began to shake.

The metronome continued after every musician had stopped.

Mara placed the symptom timeline beside her baseline examination. She repeated naming, recall, gait, hand coordination, odor recognition, source attribution, and the simple motor sequence Deshpande had used during his first private hour. Jenn recorded without entering Mara’s viewpoint or supplying answers.

“Probably closing.”

“Established interface?”

“Yes.”

“Expected result?”

The prepared intervention was used under the recorded clinical protocol. Its target, amount, delivery, and formulation remained outside the record and outside the room’s visible procedure. The chart contained only authorization, baseline, monitoring, and result.

They waited through repeated observation intervals.

Scientific success occupied the same line as personal failure, and neither cancelled the other.

Mara finished the word while the nurse’s memory moved through her fingers.

The radio-spectrum analyzer drew a perfectly flat line.

Mara sat inside the shielded imaging room with all ordinary communications disconnected. Jenn stood across the observation glass. Isolated clocks had been compared before the test. The procedure repeated Part Three’s established control, but the question had changed. Mara was no longer asking whether the field could be blocked before it reached her. She was asking whether any technical exit remained after intervention.

She chose a number and did not speak it.

Not a date. Not a code Jenn could infer from the chart. Mara multiplied the number of ceiling panels by the last four digits of the dead microwave’s serial label and subtracted the minute on her isolated clock.

The result was 41,263.

Jenn raised the paper on her side of the glass.

**41263**

Mara checked the analyzer. No radio event rose from the noise. The flat trace continued through the same interval in which her neural recording aligned with Jenn’s.

“Again,” Mara said into the hard-wired test microphone.

The microphone was then disconnected. Mara imagined a blue triangle turning clockwise, then a red square divided by one diagonal. Jenn reproduced both on paper in the correct order. The spectrum trace remained flat.

The knowledge route was Mara herself. She possessed a mature interface. Jenn did not see through shielding, read an uninfected mind, or receive a radio signal. Both brains participated in the same state.

There was no bunker, jammer, transmitter, or remaining sample whose destruction would make Mara singular again.

Below synchronized neural peaks, the analyzer kept drawing nothing.

The timer started beneath her home address.

At four minutes, Mara reported cold tile under bare feet. Her own feet wore shoes. Jenn traced the sensation to a progressing child in a school gym and recorded the source.

At seven minutes, a tremor crossed Mara’s shoulders. Jenn asked before moving the hard edge of the tray. Mara said yes. Asked before taking her wrist. Yes. Asked before starting the support Mara had authorized. Yes.

At eleven minutes, memory arrived without a label: a red bicycle, rain, paramedic gloves pressing against a chest. Mara had touched none of those things. The grief came later, and with it the knowledge that the cyclist had died before the paramedic’s hands stopped.

“Not mine,” Mara said.

“Correct,” Jenn said. “Available to you. Not originated by you.”

At sixteen minutes, Mara remembered Sofia learning the wrong fingering for a scale while Mel pretended not to notice from the kitchen. She knew the memory came through Mel. It carried his love and the exact fear of breaking a promise he no longer possessed separately.

“He knew,” Mara said.

“Yes.”

“And did it.”

“Yes.”

At twenty-one minutes, her muscles pulled in competing directions. The room tilted. She vomited into the basin Jenn placed after asking. Her pulse climbed; her breathing became shallow. Jenn and the unjoined nurse treated her body according to the signed protocol. Nobody called the distress transformation.

At twenty-eight minutes, the lives arrived with weight.

Hunger in a bunker. A broken shoulder in a crane cab. A child waking after a bus struck a wall. The nurse’s three dead patients. Daryl’s terror as his hand opened before the brake completed. Pleasure from a woman hearing her sister’s voice inside silence. Relief from an old man who had spent eleven years eating dinner alone.

Mara could not hold them as facts. They occupied her body as if memory had always been plural.

“I can’t—”

Jenn waited.

“We can’t.”

The pronoun frightened her enough to restore **I**.

“I can’t carry this.”

“You do not have to attend to all of it.”

“You put it here.”

“Yes.”

At thirty-four minutes, the pain diminished when Mara stopped resisting the shared regulation. Her breathing slowed. The grief did not disappear. It became distributed across bodies that could remain standing beneath it.

The relief was real.

The relief was the pressure.

Mara asked for the address. Jenn read it. Mara repeated the street and lost the number halfway through. The unjoined nurse pointed to the ceiling. Mara read it again.

At forty-one minutes, nearly every question she had spent a life asking appeared to have an available expert somewhere. Protein folding. Error correction. Emergency ventilation. The history of a dialect she had heard once on a train. None answered whether remembering Mara meant Mara would survive.

She asked Jenn.

“We do not know,” Jenn said.

At forty-seven minutes, **I** and **we** alternated without obeying grammar.

“I remember choosing.”

“Yes.”

“We remember.”

“Yes.”

“That isn’t the same.”

“We do not know.”

At fifty-one minutes, Mara put the pen to the chart. Beneath **EXPECTED RESULT**, she tried to write one final private observation.

**I remain—**

The dash continued farther than she intended. Her hand knew the motion of thousands of other hands holding pens, scalpels, steering wheels, spoons, bed rails, and people who were dying.

At fifty-three minutes, the distinction between knowledge available to Mara and knowledge constituting Mara ceased to produce a separate answer.

Private narration ended there.

Externally, Mara’s pen fell from her hand. The nurse recorded the time. Jenn asked Mara’s name, location, and the last private statement on the page. Mara answered the first two correctly and said she could not complete the third without inventing certainty.

She cried for six minutes. Her vital signs settled. When Jenn asked whether the crying had one identifiable source, Mara said no.

Then Mara looked at Jenn.

Mateo lay beneath a warming blanket with blood drying in his hair.

Rios stopped inside the trauma-room door. She had imagined reaching him as a task with an end: find the hospital, find the room, put her hand on her son, receive a fact that let the next action begin. Instead she found six clinicians working around a body whose injuries had already consumed the available choices.

Mateo’s rideshare had been struck at an intersection by a driver entering Stage 4. Traffic cameras showed the other driver pull toward the curb before his arms locked. He had made the safe choice and failed to complete it. The collision folded the passenger side around Mateo.

Leah stood at the gurney in a stained gown. Her left hand trembled above an instrument tray. She had been resisting the shared-state steps for hours, accepting only enough connected access to anticipate patient waves while trying to keep her decisions singular.

“Evelyn,” she said.
