# PART ONE

## FOUR NOTES

The printer was innocent.

Luis Ruiz established this in eleven minutes, although the astronomers continued behaving as if he had merely failed to locate the crime.

He stood with the access panel open and his meter clipped to a clean ground while paper accumulated in untidy strata around his shoes. Some sheets held graphs. Others held columns of four letters arranged in groups that looked like inventory codes. Someone had emptied the color tray, and four uncapped markers rolled whenever a person brushed the table.

The room smelled of dust, coffee, and people who had forgotten they possessed bodies.

“It’s not banding,” Luis said.

Nobody answered.

“Your printer,” he tried again, “is printing what you send it.”

Bob looked up from three monitors. His eyes were wet and furious from not blinking. “That’s the problem.”

Luis waited. Equipment repair taught patience. People generally arrived at the useful sentence after exhausting the sentences they enjoyed.

Bob pulled a page free from beneath Luis’s boot. Four vertical tracks crossed it in red, green, blue, and black. The marks wandered slightly to the right, stopped, then began again.

“Every seventy-eight seconds,” Bob said.

Luis inspected the rollers once more. He did this for Bob, not for the printer.

Outside, dawn made the dishes of the array look flat and artificial, like props waiting for the sky to be added later. One by one they moved. Their motors were too distant to hear through the glass.

“So it repeats,” Luis said.

“Exactly.”

“Then you only need to print one.”

Bob stared at him.

Luis closed the access panel. On the work order he wrote NO FAULT FOUND, because the alternate diagnosis was not available in the drop-down menu.

* * *

At 6:14 a.m., Mara Velez was cutting the burnt edge off a piece of toast so that she could pretend it had not burned.

Her husband watched from the kitchen table.

“You know the carbon goes deeper than the visible part,” Tom said.

“That is not how toast works.”

“It is how shame works.”

She turned the bread over. The other side was worse.

Her phone vibrated beside the butter dish. The number had a Washington area code and the antiseptic confidence of a call that would not leave a message.

Tom raised his coffee in acknowledgment. “Your real husband.”

“My real husband knows my time zone.”

She answered on the fourth vibration.

The woman on the line asked if she was alone.

Mara looked at Tom. He had already lowered his eyes to the crossword. Seventeen years together had taught him how to create privacy without leaving a room. It was one of the reasons she loved him and one of the reasons she sometimes treated him as furniture.

“No,” Mara said.

The woman asked if she could be.

Mara carried the phone into the pantry and closed the door between the rice and the recycling.

Forty minutes later she was packing a bag.

“Biodefense?” Tom asked.

“I didn’t say that.”

“You packed the blue suit.”

“The blue suit is not a biosafety indicator.”

“It’s the suit you wear when you need generals to think you own an iron.”

Mara laid a pair of running shoes on top of the suit. She had not run since February.

Tom leaned against the doorframe. “How long?”

“Two days.”

“Actual days or government days?”

“I don’t know.”

She waited for him to congratulate her. Saying the three words without qualification was, in their marriage, an act of nudity.

He only nodded. “I’ll throw out the toast.”

* * *

The secure conference room had twelve chairs, eighteen people, and a wall display that made every graph look guilty.

Mara was given a badge with no agency name and escorted past two doors that required different fingers. Nobody told her where she was. A framed photograph in the corridor showed a former secretary of agriculture inspecting sorghum, which narrowed the possibilities in an unhelpful way.

Bob and another astronomer presented first. They had cleaned themselves up and become less convincing in the process.

The signal originated in Cygnus. Its apparent drift matched a source roughly six hundred light-years away. It repeated every seventy-eight seconds. Four pulse positions carried the information. Terrestrial interference had been excluded by separate observatories and by a list of tests Bob recited with the injured speed of a man forced to defend gravity.

An intelligence analyst asked whether the sequence might be encrypted.

“Everything is encrypted until you know what it means,” Bob said.

“That’s not what encrypted means,” the analyst said.

“Then no.”

Mara studied the four columns. Their frequencies were irrelevant, she thought. Frequency was the envelope. Position was the alphabet.

She asked for the raw stream.

The analyst said it could not leave the room.

“Neither can I, apparently.”

Someone found her a terminal.

For three hours Mara tested the sort of patterns intelligent beings were supposed to send to strangers: primes, ratios, geometry, atomic spectra. She found none. She converted the four states into base four and obtained long, graceless numbers. She looked for compression headers, checksums, images, redundant language. Nothing behaved like a greeting.

At 11:52, a catering trolley arrived. A man in a good suit began moving legal pads to make room for sandwiches.

“Please don’t put turkey on the data,” Mara said.

The man froze with a tray in his hands.

“Sorry. Davis Taffler.” His badge identified him as an under secretary in a branch of Agriculture long enough to require two lines. “I was told livestock might become relevant.”

“Livestock is always relevant if you wait long enough.”

He considered that, decided it was probably a joke, and distributed the sandwiches by dietary label.

Mara picked up a packet of mustard. On the back, four blocks of text repeated in four languages.

She looked at the signal again.

Not four numbers. Four bases.

She assigned the four states to a biological alphabet, then tried every permutation. Most produced nonsense. One produced long patterns with the internal repetition and variation of biological instructions without resembling anything in the databases.

Mara forgot the sandwich.

“How certain are you?” the intelligence analyst asked twenty minutes later.

“Of which part?”

“The part where the aliens faxed us a disease.”

“Nobody faxed anything. And I didn’t say disease.”

“You said virus.”

“I said virus-like.”

“What is the operational difference?”

“At present? Whether we sound stupid in the report.”

The analyst pinched the bridge of his nose.

Mara enlarged one of the repeating regions. “A virus is not a bag of evil. It is information with a delivery problem. This may be information that expects a cell to solve the delivery problem for it.”

Davis, still holding two unclaimed sandwiches, asked, “Whose cell?”

That was the first useful question anyone had asked.

* * *

The signal acquired a project name, then a different project name after the first was discovered to be an existing naval radar. Eleven institutions received fragments. No institution received enough to understand what its fragment helped build.

Mara spent the next six weeks in rooms with filtered air and bad chairs.

The sequence was too orderly to be random and too unfamiliar to annotate conventionally. Every team received a different fragment and returned the same uncomfortable verdict: it behaved like biological instruction without matching a known organism. Long regions appeared to specify architecture rather than ordinary metabolism. One model predicted that normal warning signs might arrive late. The immunologist who presented that result quietly took off her glasses.

“It could be active before we know what to call active,” she said.

“Meaning?” asked the project director.

“Meaning our observations may lag behind the event.”

Nobody wrote for several seconds. Then everybody did.

The safest proposal was observation. Publish nothing, synthesize nothing, continue modeling.

Mara agreed with it for an entire afternoon.

That evening Tom called from a grocery store because the brand of olives she liked had changed its label. He held two jars to the camera while shoppers steered around him.

“The one on the left,” she said.

“They are both on my left.”

“Turn the phone around.”

He turned himself around instead.

For ten minutes they discussed olives because they could not discuss why she had missed his cardiology appointment. His tests were normal. He had texted that fact. She had responded with a heart, then spent two hours wondering whether a heart was adequate and another hour doing nothing about it.

“When are you home?” he asked.

“When we know whether to build it.”

“Build what?”

Mara said nothing.

Tom watched her through the small screen. “Right.”

After the call, she returned to the observation-only proposal and found the flaw she had been looking for.

The original radio data had already been copied to three civilian observatories before classification. Two graduate students had taken portions home. A compressed waterfall plot appeared in a private astronomy forum. Given enough time, somebody else would see the biological mapping. The choice was not between synthesis and permanent safety. It was between controlled synthesis now and uncontrolled synthesis later.

She wrote the argument in twelve pages, reduced it to four, and presented it the next morning in seven minutes.

Dr. Havel from biosecurity listened without interrupting. “You’re converting uncertainty into urgency.”

“The uncertainty already has a clock.”

“A clock you infer.”

“A clock created by information access. The signal is in the sky.”

“So is ultraviolet radiation. We don’t manufacture extra.”

“We manufacture it every time we need to understand what it does.”

“In shielded boxes.”

“Yes.”

“With organisms that evolved here.”

“Until now, that described all organisms.”

Havel leaned back. “Tell me the sentence you want in the authorization.”

Mara knew what he was offering. Not agreement. Ownership.

She said, “A staged synthesis inside the sealed system, with independent review before anything leaves it.”

“No scale-up.”

“Yes.”

“No animal work without a separate vote.”

“Yes.”

“No work capable of producing a transmissible sample without another approval.”

Mara hesitated.

The silence altered the room.

Havel’s expression did not change. “That was the easiest condition.”

“It may make the result uninterpretable.”

“Then we accept an uninterpretable result.”

“And when another laboratory doesn’t?”

There it was: the argument that made caution resemble negligence.

Mara saw it work around the table. People who had arrived prepared to forbid the experiment began imagining unknown people performing it badly. Her scenario colonized their judgment one reasonable step at a time.

The authorization passed with conditions. Synthesis would occur inside the purpose-built sealed system, under a protocol Mara was not permitted to carry out herself. Animal work and any later expansion required separate approvals.

Mara signed first.

In the corridor Davis caught up with her, walking quickly without appearing to hurry.

“Congratulations,” he said.

“That’s a strange response.”

“I wasn’t sure what else one says.”

“You say the decision was appropriately documented.”

“Was it?”

She stopped.

Davis looked alarmed by his own question. “I mean, I’m sure it was. There were a lot of documents.”

Mara resumed walking. “That isn’t the same thing.”

“No,” he said. “I suppose it isn’t.”

* * *

The first synthesis run took place at 2:10 in the morning because instrument time was cheaper then.

Mara watched through glass with Deshpande, who had joined the project during the third review and immediately improved it by refusing to be impressed.

“You could go home,” he said.

“So could you.”

“I am avoiding my brother-in-law.”

“I’m providing oversight.”

“Your oversight is fogging the window.”

Inside the clean room, a technician loaded four sealed reservoirs identified only by barcode. The liquid-handling robot woke with a small domestic hum. Its head moved, checked pressure, and paused while the software compared the labels.

Green status lights traveled down the line.

Mara’s phone vibrated. A photograph from Tom showed the olives on their kitchen counter beside two empty plates. He had captioned it DATE NIGHT.

She typed: **Soon.**

The robot positioned itself above the first plate.

Four pipettes descended toward four waiting wells.
