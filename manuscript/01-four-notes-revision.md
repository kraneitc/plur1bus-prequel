# FOUR NOTES

Four toner cartridges stood open on the floor while Luis Ruiz proved the printer innocent.

It took eleven minutes. The astronomers continued behaving as though he had failed to locate the crime.

Paper covered the operations room in layers. Some sheets held the usual gray waterfalls of radio noise. Others carried vertical marks in red, green, blue and black, added by hand after somebody decided the printer could no longer be trusted with color. The marks drifted right, stopped, and started again. Four uncapped pens rolled along the table whenever anyone leaned against it.

Luis removed a strip of crumpled paper from the feed path and held it up.

“This is the jam.”

Nobody looked.

“The jam is why it stopped printing. It is not why it printed what you sent it.”

Bob Kaplan raised his head from three monitors. He had the wet, furious eyes of a man who had recently discovered blinking was optional.

“Run the test page,” he said.

Luis already had. He placed it on top of the nearest stack: four perfect bars, each ending where the manufacturer intended. Cyan, magenta, yellow, black. No banding, no offset, no evidence that the machine had invented a message from the sky.

“Again,” Bob said.

“It will be the same printer.”

“Please.”

Luis ran it again because equipment repair was mostly the art of proving the same thing until the owner could bear it. The page emerged warm and flawless.

Outside the windows, dawn flattened the radio dishes into pale shapes waiting for the rest of the landscape. One moved, then another. Their motors could not be heard through the glass.

An astronomer named Priya drew four new marks on a fresh sheet. She checked the clock taped above her terminal.

“Seventy-eight,” she said.

Bob moved to her screen. The room tightened around whatever he saw there.

Luis clipped the access panel shut. “Seventy-eight what?”

“Seconds.”

“Since?”

“The last one.”

On Priya's monitor, four narrow pulse positions rose out of the noise. They vanished. A timer in the corner began counting again.

Luis waited through the interval because everyone else did.

At seventy-eight seconds, the four positions returned carrying a different arrangement. Priya marked them in color. Bob pulled the page toward himself and laid it beside the previous page. The sequences differed. Their spacing did not.

“So it repeats,” Luis said.

“The carrier repeats,” Priya said. “The content changes.”

“Then why print all of it?”

Neither astronomer answered. Bob was sorting pages across the floor, aligning the hand-drawn marks into longer columns.

Luis opened the electronic work order. The available causes were mechanical wear, electrical fault, driver error, consumable failure and user damage. He selected **NO FAULT FOUND**.

Behind him, Priya stopped marking the next recurrence.

“We've got four states,” she said.

Bob stood. “Call the university.”

“Which department?”

He looked down at the coloured pages as though one of them might have printed the answer.

“Find me somebody who knows what four can mean.”

The repaired printer idled beside them. Nobody noticed.

* * *

Mara Velez muted the graduate student before the student had technically earned it.

“The model demonstrates a statistically meaningful—”

His mouth continued moving on the left side of her screen. On the right, a secure window displayed four white traces against black and a timer counting down from seventy-eight.

Mara slid her office door shut with one foot. The student had arranged his results into four colors because the department template offered four colors. She knew this. The coincidence still irritated her.

An observatory administrator filled the top square of the secure call. Two astronomers occupied the square beneath him, cropped at the shoulders by a camera pointed too low.

“Is that the complete capture?” Mara asked.

“It's the complete transcription,” Bob said.

“I asked about the capture.”

“The raw stream is large.”

“So is the universe.”

The administrator cleared his throat. “Doctor Velez, we were advised that you consult across information theory and molecular systems.”

“Who advised you?”

“The Department of Energy.”

That did not answer her question. It did answer several others.

She unmuted her student long enough to say, “Send me the residuals and remove the phrase statistically meaningful from every slide where you have not stated meaningful to whom.”

“Now?” he asked.

“Before your committee discovers adjectives.”

She ended the university call.

On the secure window, the pulse arrangement returned. Mara ignored the astronomers' coloured transcription and requested a short segment of raw data, plus independent captures and the clock source for each instrument.

Bob's expression suggested she had asked for the dish.

“We have already excluded local interference,” he said.

“Then it should survive being excluded again.”

The files arrived one at a time. Mara checked the recurrence against the observatories' separate clocks. The interval held. She checked whether the four positions might be one event arriving through multipath distortion. They changed independently. She asked whether orientation, weather or pointing correction altered the pattern. Nothing in the logs moved with it.

The administrator tried to bring the conversation back to language.

“The working hypothesis is a four-symbol code.”

“A code is what we call a pattern after deciding someone meant us to read it.”

“Do you have a better noun?”

“Not yet.”

She divided the stream at the recurring interval, then at the smaller internal spacings. The four states did not behave like letters in ordinary text. Their long-range repetition was too structured in some places and too uneven in others. Blocks returned with small variations, nested inside larger blocks. There were stretches that looked less like statements than instructions with conserved parts.

Mara enlarged one region. She felt the idea before she trusted it, which was the wrong order and therefore worth distrusting.

Four states. Repeated architecture. Variation without the statistical habits of language.

The timer reached zero. Another arrangement appeared.

“Has anyone tried a biochemical alphabet?” she asked.

Bob leaned closer to his camera. “Meaning?”

Mara assigned the four observed states abstract labels. She did not decide which state corresponded to which base; the mapping could wait. What mattered was whether any assignment produced the layered redundancies and instruction-like regions expected of biological information.

One did.

Not cleanly. Not in a family she recognized. But the structure stopped looking arbitrary.

Mara did not trust the relief that followed. She asked Priya to prepare three comparison sets: one from a different interval of the capture, one with the state labels reassigned, and one whose internal order had been scrambled. Priya was not to tell her which was which.

“You think you're seeing what you want?” Bob asked.

“I think wanting is cheap.”

The sets arrived without labels. Mara ran the same coarse comparisons over each. One retained its nested repetitions across distant portions of the capture. One produced short accidental patterns that disappeared when the window moved. One was uniformly graceless.

She selected the first.

Priya revealed it as the untouched interval under the biochemical mapping.

That still did not prove the mapping. It proved only that the structure survived a small attempt to embarrass it.

Mara asked for an interval from the second observatory, one nobody in Bob's room had transcribed. The same conserved regions returned around different internal content. Independent instrument, independent clock, no shared printer and no opportunity for the coloured pens to create agreement.

On her desk, the muted graduate student sent a message asking whether he should reschedule.

Mara typed **YES** and did not send it. Reschedule implied she knew what would happen next.

The administrator said, “Doctor?”

Mara should have asked for an independent team. She should have written the result down before giving the room a word it could never return.

“Nucleotide,” she said.

Every face in the call grid went still.

“You're saying it's DNA?” Priya asked.

“No.”

“RNA?”

“I am saying the four states behave more usefully as a nucleotide alphabet than as language. That is not the same claim.”

The administrator reached beyond his camera. The public-network cable came free from the wall. One of the video squares vanished, then returned through a slower secure relay.

Mara watched the student presentation disappear beneath a classification banner.

By the time she was permitted to call her department chair, somebody else had already canceled her afternoon.

* * *

The observatory gave visitors padded envelopes for their phones. The envelopes had been purchased for failed disk drives and were just wide enough to make every device look damaged.

Mara wrote her name across the seal. An intelligence liaison named Grace Wu watched the ink dry before placing the envelope in a numbered tray.

“Laptop?” Grace asked.

“Office.”

“Watch?”

Mara held up her bare wrist.

“Other storage media?”

“Working memory.”

Grace did not smile. “That leaves with you.”

“Then your procedure has a gap.”

“The procedure has an assumption about your patriotism.”

“Less comforting.”

Mara had been driven from the university without being told whether she was under escort. The same unmarked car would return her if she finished before it acquired a second purpose.

Inside the conference room, the paper stacks from dawn had been converted into an evidence display. Each page carried a time, instrument identifier and initials. Luis's work order sat at the corner beneath a clear sleeve: **NO FAULT FOUND**.

Grace opened a government packet. The first page described the signal as an extraterrestrial four-state transmission of unknown intent. The second page contained the working transcription.

Mara read five lines and stopped.

“This block is wrong.”

Bob folded his arms. “It was checked twice.”

“Against what?”

“The printed record.”

“The printed record breaks here.” Mara placed one finger on a page boundary. “This recurrence starts one pulse late. Somebody continued the color columns at the top margin instead of the timestamp.”

Grace said, “Can you demonstrate that without changing the record?”

Mara asked for one raw interval on an isolated terminal. Grace logged the request, Bob witnessed it and Priya retrieved the segment. Mara aligned the clock markers, reconstructed the missing state and wrote the corrected block on a transparency laid over the government page.

The nested pattern resumed.

Bob took off his glasses. He did not apologize; he looked too relieved.

Grace marked the packet **SUPERSEDED** and began a new chain-of-custody line.

“I need the full raw stream,” Mara said.

“You need the portion relevant to your finding.”

“You don't know which portion that is.”

“Neither do you.”

“That is why I need it.”

Grace considered her, then looked at the corrected block. “You can access it here. No copies leave.”

It was more than Mara had been given and less than the work required. She accepted before Grace could improve the offer downward.

The secrecy agreement arrived while the data loaded. It prohibited disclosure of the signal, the location, the agencies present, the biological interpretation and the existence of the agreement. Mara initialed three pages. On the fourth, a secure phone began ringing behind Grace.

Grace answered, listened, then held it out.

“Dr. Deshpande.”

Mara signed the final page without finishing the paragraph above her name.

Deshpande's first words were, “Please tell me you did not call it a virus.”

“I called it nucleotide-like.”

“That is two escape hatches in one adjective.”

“You have not seen it.”

“Then show me enough to object properly.”

* * *

By four that afternoon, the word **VIRUS** had appeared on a whiteboard in a provisional biology room.

Deshpande covered it with laboratory tape and wrote **NOUN PENDING** across the tape.

“Whose word?” Mara asked.

“Deputy director.”

“Which deputy director?”

“He did not stay long enough to become specific.”

The room belonged to a federal laboratory that had not existed on Mara's itinerary that morning. It contained two secured workstations, one unreliable coffee machine and six people who had been told only enough to make their specialties dangerous to one another.

Mara and Deshpande worked from derived representations rather than the raw sequence. Grace sat behind them to ensure the distinction remained meaningful.

The other specialists arrived by secure link. A structural biologist saw repeated components but would not call them proteins. A virologist objected that the information was too architecturally elaborate for the category being used in the invitations. A computational chemist said the predicted folding made no sense, then asked for a larger derived region and said it made less sense with greater confidence.

Nobody agreed on function. Everyone agreed the order was expensive: too much long-range structure to dismiss as a random four-state broadcast, too many altered repetitions to call it a simple beacon.

First they tested the least alarming explanations. A human artifact. A formatting artifact. A known biological family disguised by an incorrect mapping. A non-coding pattern whose apparent structure arose from the recurrence window.

Each explanation survived long enough to be written on the left side of the board. None survived the next comparison.

Deshpande moved each failed explanation to the right side rather than erasing it.

“Why keep them?” Grace asked.

“Because tomorrow somebody will suggest them again with more authority.”

The right side filled. Human contamination failed against the independent observatories. Formatting artifact failed when the raw intervals were aligned. A familiar biological family failed because every useful comparison required ignoring a different essential feature. Non-coding signal failed because the conserved and variable regions nested too consistently across the capture.

Mara objected when the virologist tried to infer a host from those regions.

“You don't have a host interaction,” she said.

“I have a region that could imply one.”

“Could imply is not evidence of.”

Deshpande looked at her over the coffee he had refused to drink. “That is almost cautious.”

“I'm conserving it.”

Deshpande refused to let the failures accumulate into certainty.

“Unknown does not become extraterrestrial biology merely because we run out of terrestrial folders.”

“The source is extraterrestrial.”

“The signal appears to originate six hundred light-years away. The interpretation is sitting in this room drinking bad coffee.”

“You're not drinking it.”

“My caution has limits.”

Mara expanded a conceptual map of the instruction-like regions. They did not resemble ordinary metabolism. Long portions appeared architectural: repeated components, altered components, nested constraints. She could not say what would be built, only that the information behaved as though construction mattered more than replication alone.

By late afternoon, RNA had returned as the least-wrong working model. Not a known viral genome and not a complete organism: an RNA-like instruction system whose informational burden appeared concentrated on making a molecular structure inside cells. Every part of that sentence remained provisional except the discomfort it caused.

Deshpande leaned forward until his tie touched the desk.

“No known family,” he said.

“Agreed.”

“No known host.”

“Agreed.”

“No evidence it functions if embodied.”

Mara disliked the verb because it made the next question sound ceremonial.

“No evidence either way.”

Grace needed a designation for the review packet. Every working noun had acquired assumptions by noon.

“Four Notes is already in the astronomy chain,” she said. “Virus is disputed. Sequence is too broad. Give me something that does not tell the recipient what to conclude.”

Deshpande wrote **S-78** beneath the taped-over noun.

“S for signal,” he said. “Seventy-eight for the recurrence. It identifies the file, not the organism.”

“There is no organism,” Mara said.

“Good. The name has improved the evidence.”

Grace entered S-78 into the subject line and added **PROVISIONAL DESIGNATION** twice.

Grace looked up. “Embodied how?”

Deshpande pointed at **NOUN PENDING**. “That is the question the noun keeps trying to answer for us.”

The safest claim they could support was also the least comforting: the four-state signal contained organized biological information unlike any category they could identify.

It did not say disease. It did not say benign. It did not say message.

Grace routed the conclusion to national security and biosafety. A new meeting invitation appeared on Mara's workstation before the old one had ended.

The subject line read **FOREIGN BIOLOGICAL MATERIAL**.

There was no material.

* * *

Tom served two plates and set Mara's secure phone in the space between them.

“Your guest,” he said.

The phone was dark. Its black screen reflected the pendant light and half of Mara's face.

“It has to stay where I can hear it.”

“I assumed it preferred the chair.”

He sat opposite her. On his side of the table, the mail had been sorted into bills, recycling and an envelope from the cardiology clinic. On hers, nothing had been left that might require an answer.

A small bowl of green olives sat beside her plate. Mara tasted one and looked at the jar on the counter.

“They changed the label,” Tom said.

“This isn't the same brand.”

“Same barcode.”

“Barcodes survive acquisitions.”

“I kept the receipt.”

“That proves you bought the wrong olives.”

Tom reached behind the fruit bowl and produced the old empty jar. He had compared the ingredients, distributor and tiny address printed along the bottom. They matched.

“You investigated dinner,” Mara said.

“You were unavailable for consultation.”

She ate another olive. It was the same. Tom did not require the admission.

Mara cut into the fish. Tom had cooked it exactly as she liked and served it ten minutes after it should have been eaten.

“I canceled Taos,” she said.

“I know.”

“How?”

“The hotel emailed both of us.”

“I was going to tell you.”

“Before Friday?”

“It is Tuesday.”

“Good start.”

He ate. Mara waited for the sharper version of the conversation. Tom had become skilled at refusing to supply one.

“There is a review,” she said.

“Of your work?”

“Related work.”

“In New Mexico?”

“I can't say.”

“You are in New Mexico.”

“That's not classified.”

“I appreciate the disclosure.”

The phone remained dark between them. Mara could have moved it to the counter. That would not have made the meal private; it would only have made her less responsive when it rang.

Tom opened the cardiology envelope and slid the result across the table. Normal. He had already told her by text. She had sent a red heart and then returned to the sequence.

“I am glad,” she said.

“It was less suspenseful in the room.”

“I said I was sorry.”

“You sent punctuation.”

“A heart is not punctuation.”

“It ended the sentence.”

Mara folded the result along its existing crease. The phone lit beneath her hand. **SECURE MESSAGE RECEIVED**. She did not open it.

Tom watched her not open it.

“Is the work dangerous?” he asked.

There were answers the agreement prohibited. There were answers it permitted that would frighten him for no useful reason. Mara found the narrow fact that could cross the table intact.

“The laboratory isn't doing anything.”

“Right now?”

“At all.”

“Then why did you cancel the weekend?”

“Because the question is whether it should.”

Tom placed his fork beside the plate. “Should what?”

Mara looked at the secure phone.

He nodded once. Not agreement. Receipt.

After dinner he washed the pans while she read the message. The first biosafety session had been scheduled for seven in the morning. Attached were two competing protocols and a note that all discussion would occur inside the facility.

In bed, Tom turned away from her without performing anger. His breathing lengthened. Mara inserted one earpiece and played the sanitized audio representation the astronomers had prepared for review.

Four tones. Silence. Four tones in a new order.

The interval lasted seventy-eight seconds. Long enough for her to remove the earpiece.

She did not.

* * *

At seven the next morning, Dr. Havel placed two folders on the conference table.

The thin folder said **OBSERVE**. The thick folder said **CONSTRUCT**.

Havel was a risk officer with the build of a man who had spent his career being underestimated in doorways. He pushed the thin folder toward Mara and the thick one toward the center, where nobody would mistake it for a personal proposal.

“The word construct is prejudicial,” Mara said.

“Good. It describes a prejudicial difference.”

The observation-only protocol was rigorous. Independent observatories would continue collecting the transmission. Separate teams would test the biological mapping. No full sequence would circulate. No laboratory would produce a physical analogue. Derived findings would move through controlled channels; raw captures would remain compartmented.

It answered every known risk by declining to create the unknown one.

Havel made the procedure physical before asking for votes. The complete raw capture remained at the observatory. Biology teams received only the derived regions necessary to test a stated question. Results returned through a review desk that could detect when separate requests, taken together, approached reconstruction. Every ninety days, the panel would decide whether new observation justified widening access.

“What ends it?” Mara asked.

“The protocol?”

“Observation.”

“A conclusion that the mapping is unsupported. A better interpretation. A decision that further work has no public value.”

“Or none of those.”

“Correct.”

The thin folder allowed the signal to remain a permanent object of study without ever becoming material. Several people in the room found that a strength.

Mara read to the end twice, hoping competence would reveal its own flaw.

Deshpande closed his copy. “This is the correct first protocol.”

She looked at him. “First implies a second.”

“If evidence earns one.”

“What evidence can observation produce about function?”

“Perhaps none.”

“That does not concern you?”

“It concerns me enough not to manufacture the only known sample.”

The structural biologist agreed with him. So did the virologist, who disliked the invitation's noun but disliked creating evidence for it more. Grace supported observation because the data could remain inside a chain the state already knew how to protect.

Mara asked what happened if an independent observatory found the same mapping.

“We share the risk assessment,” Havel said.

“And if they don't ask?”

“Then we address the event that exists, not the one you can imagine.”

It was a professionally sound answer. Mara heard it as a door closing before she had reached the room behind it.

Around the table, the other specialists found reasons to study the thin folder. They were not cowards. Mara had watched one of them stop a vaccine program after years of work because a control result would not behave. Another had once closed his own laboratory for six months over an airflow discrepancy nobody else could reproduce. Their caution was not a costume put on for the aliens.

Havel went around the table. The observation protocol accumulated votes.

When Mara's turn came, she said, “Not yet.”

“That is not one of the options,” Havel said.

“Then your options are missing time.”

He waited.

Mara did not have the argument. She had only the resistance to letting a well-made folder close over a question she had named.

“I want forty-eight hours to submit a minority position.”

“Based on what evidence?”

“Based on finding out what we failed to count.”

“You may have the time. The interim rule is observation only.”

Havel collected the votes. Deshpande helped stack the papers but left Mara's notes where they were. The thin folder closed over them.

There was no sample in the world. The safest exit remained open.

Mara went looking for a reason to close it.

* * *

The secure workroom had no windows and a world map that was not supposed to connect to anything.

Mara connected pushpins with string because the approved software could display classified sites or civilian sites, not both at once. The result looked less like analysis than an investigation conducted by someone with a stationery budget.

The source region was visible from much of the northern hemisphere. Professional radio observatories had the sensitivity to recover the transmission cleanly. Smaller instruments could recover portions. Amateur arrays had already recorded the carrier without recognizing the content.

Bob appeared on a secure video link. Behind him, the repaired printer continued producing pages.

“Before classification,” Mara said, “who received raw data?”

“Define received.”

“Had access sufficient to copy.”

“Staff.”

“How many?”

“I would have to count.”

“Count.”

He looked wounded. “We were verifying an astronomical event. We did not issue a secrecy oath before opening the telescope.”

“I know. Count anyway.”

The number grew. On-shift astronomers. Graduate students. The second observatory asked to confirm. A systems administrator who had mirrored a drive. Two civilian partners who had been sent partial captures before the source distance was established.

Priya joined the call and admitted that a compressed waterfall image had appeared for nine minutes in a private astronomy forum. It had been removed after classification.

“Downloaded?” Mara asked.

“The forum doesn't expose that.”

“Screenshots?”

“It was an image.”

“Of course it was.”

The image did not contain the full data. It did contain enough structure to reward curiosity. Mara placed a pin for every independent route by which the signal could be recovered again without any government file leaving custody.

The map filled.

Grace entered, took one look at the string and said, “This room will now have to be photographed before we can remove the map.”

“Good.”

“That was not approval.”

“It was documentation.”

Mara drafted a dissemination assessment. She separated what had already escaped from what was simply recoverable from the sky. The distinction mattered legally and not at all to the next competent person who noticed four states behaving like biology.

By midnight she had built the argument she lacked that morning.

The signal was not a specimen secured by the state. It was a broadcast. The government could forbid its own laboratories from embodying the information. It could not make the information unavailable. Observation-only safety depended on every unknown observer making the same choice forever.

Deshpande arrived carrying two paper cups and read the assessment standing up.

He stopped at the sentence Mara had rewritten six times: **CONTROLLED KNOWLEDGE MAY BE SAFER THAN UNCONTROLLED FIRST USE.**

“May be,” he said.

“That is the honest verb.”

“It is also doing most of the work.”

“Do you disagree?”

“I disagree with how much you enjoy an argument once it becomes difficult to refuse.”

“That is not an answer.”

Deshpande circled the sentence.

“No,” he said. “I don't disagree.”

Mara waited for satisfaction. What arrived was relief, which she mistook for the same thing.

* * *

Jenn carried an empty transport vessel through the first containment door at 8:10 the next morning.

Six observers followed her progress on separate monitors. The rehearsal existed to make every handoff visible before anything biological existed to be handed.

“Seal one,” Jenn said.

The technician at the control desk repeated it. “Seal one confirmed.”

The outer door locked behind her. A status panel verified the boundary. Jenn waited until the inner indicator changed before moving the vessel from the marked shelf to the transfer cart.

The vessel was stainless steel, barcoded and empty. Everyone watched it as though danger had mass.

Mara stood beside Deshpande and Havel behind the observation glass. She followed the rehearsal sheet: receipt, identity check, sealed transfer, independent verification, remote handling, observation, stop conditions, destruction authority. The protocol described custody and decisions. The proposed construction itself remained inside a black box labeled **AUTHORIZED AUTOMATED PROCESS**.

Jenn reached the second boundary and read the vessel identifier aloud.

“Wrong,” she said.

The control technician looked at his screen. “Identifier matches.”

“I didn't say it didn't.”

“Then what is wrong?”

Jenn held up the paper incident log. “At this door I have the carrier and the log.”

“For twelve seconds.”

“For one second. If the carrier is wrong, I can change the log. If the log is wrong, I can change the carrier. Same person holds the event and the record.”

Havel pressed the intercom. “The cameras preserve both.”

“The cameras preserve what happened,” Jenn said. “They don't stop it.”

The countdown on the rehearsal screen continued. Every reserved minute in the suite had been assigned a cost by somebody who would never enter it.

Mara read the handoff again. The protocol required two-person verification at receipt and at the automated enclosure. Between them, Jenn crossed one interlocked space alone.

“Pause the clock,” Mara said.

The control technician looked to Havel. Havel looked at the empty vessel.

“Pause,” he said.

The countdown stopped.

Jenn proposed that the control technician hold the record through the interlock and that any technician carrying the vessel receive independent stop authority, without waiting for a principal investigator to decide whether the deviation was important.

One of the observers objected. “You are asking a technician to halt a national-security operation.”

“I'm asking the person in the room to stop moving when the room is wrong.”

Deshpande said, “Those are the same request.”

Mara expected him to oppose the authority. He was watching her instead.

“Add it,” she said.

Havel revised the procedure. The control technician took custody of the log, repeated the identifier and restarted the clock. Jenn carried the empty vessel through the remaining doors, placed it in the remote enclosure and returned by the designated route.

The rehearsal then introduced a simulated fault.

One boundary indicator changed from green to amber. The procedure gave Jenn three permitted actions: hold position, verify the displayed boundary and call a stop if the mismatch persisted. It did not permit her to inspect the enclosure, open the vessel or accept a verbal assurance that the sensor was probably wrong.

The control technician said, “Known simulation.”

Jenn did not move.

“You heard me?”

“The room didn't.”

She read the amber indicator into the record. The control technician confirmed it. Havel tested the remote stop and verified that the destruction authority had become available without being exercised. Only after the simulated boundary returned green and a second observer confirmed it did Jenn complete the route.

The delay accumulated on Mara's screen. The system she had promised was now wasting her reserved time for the precise reason it existed: the people who designed it did not outrank the amber light.

The sequence finished nineteen minutes late.

At the last boundary, Jenn waited for the release indicator. It stayed red.

Nobody spoke. She did not try the handle.

The panel completed its check and turned green.

Jenn stepped out.

The safety officer stamped the rehearsal sheet **FEASIBLE**.

Below the stamp, in smaller type, a box marked **SAFE** remained empty.

* * *

Six agencies brought six title slides to the authorization hearing.

One called the matter an anomalous astrophysical transmission. Another called it non-terrestrial bioinformation. The intelligence slide said **FOREIGN SIGNAL EVENT**, which prompted the deputy secretary chairing the meeting to ask whether a source six hundred light-years away counted as foreign jurisdiction.

Grace said, “Not under the definitions in our current authorities.”

“Do we have definitions that include it?”

“Not in authorities I can discuss here.”

The deputy secretary considered the room. “I would like the minutes to reflect that I asked.”

Davis Taffler adjusted the nameplate in front of him so it faced outward. His title occupied two lines and ended with the Department of Agriculture. He had been included because any future animal work would cross several areas nobody wanted to discover after authorization.

Mara's slide said **FOUR NOTES** and contained no agency seal.

Havel presented first. He did not dramatize the unknown or minimize it. Observation could establish recurrence, independent reception, internal structure and perhaps additional mappings. Synthesis could establish function. It would also create the only known physical instance of whatever the signal described.

“There is a moral difference,” he said, “between receiving an object and choosing to embody its instructions.”

The deputy secretary looked at Mara. “Do you disagree?”

“No.”

Several people glanced at one another. They had prepared for a cleaner dispute.

Mara placed Luis's **NO FAULT FOUND** work order beneath the map of receiving sites.

“I disagree that we control reception.”

She walked the room through the custody history. Multiple observatories could recover the carrier. Partial captures had moved through civilian systems before classification. No file needed to leak for another observer to collect the same broadcast. The four-state biological mapping was difficult, but difficulty was not containment.

An intelligence official said, “We can request cooperation from allied facilities.”

“You can request cooperation from the facilities you know about.”

“We can monitor publication.”

“The first sign of synthesis may not be a publication.”

Havel folded his hands. “That is an argument for controlling information more aggressively.”

Grace said, “The dissemination assessment already requires expanded classification.”

Mara looked at her. “It also says classification cannot recover the sky.”

The deputy secretary asked what synthesis would answer that modeling could not.

Mara resisted the urge to say function. The word was too large to be useful.

“Whether the instruction-like regions produce any coherent biological effect when physically realized,” she said. “Whether our mapping is a projection. Whether the result persists, fails immediately, or does nothing. Observation can refine the question. It cannot observe a material we decline to create.”

Davis raised one finger, then lowered it when nobody noticed. Mara did.

“Mr. Taffler?”

“If it does nothing, how long before doing nothing is accepted as the result?”

“We would establish predetermined endpoints.”

“And if it does something we did not think to measure?”

“Then the containment system and independent observation matter more than our hypotheses.”

He wrote that down. Mara could not tell whether she had reassured him.

The senior science adviser proposed a multinational accord: preserve observation only until allied governments agreed on shared controls and no single laboratory could create a fait accompli.

It was a responsible exit. It spread authority, reduced unilateral error and left the present world unchanged.

“How long?” Mara asked.

“As long as agreement requires.”

“During which every participant learns there is something to race toward.”

“That assumes bad faith.”

“It assumes prestige.”

The adviser sat back. “Including yours?”

“Yes.”

Mara felt Deshpande look at her. She kept her eyes on the adviser.

“Including mine. That is why the work should be bounded by a system that can stop it, not by the belief that every interested person will choose not to begin.”

Havel opened the thick protocol.

“A system designed by the people asking permission to use it.”

“A system revised by the technician who will carry the vessel, the safety office that can halt the room, and reviewers who oppose the work.”

“After you supplied the premise that makes refusal sound reckless.”

“Refusal has consequences whether I describe them or not.”

Deshpande slid Mara's dissemination assessment toward the center of the table. Her sentence remained circled.

“I supported observation only yesterday,” he said. “I would still prefer a world in which observation keeps the information nonphysical. Dr. Velez has shown that our protocol cannot create that world. It can create only a delay whose endpoint we do not control.”

He did not say *our argument*. He did not mention that he had circled the sentence. He left the ownership with Mara and made the room take it seriously.

The deputy secretary turned to her. “Can you guarantee containment?”

“No.”

The room became quiet enough for the ventilation to separate into individual vents.

“Can anyone?” he asked.

“No.”

“Then why authorize?”

“Because uncertainty is not reduced by assigning the first physical result to an unknown laboratory with unknown controls. We can learn under procedures we can audit, stop and revise. I cannot guarantee safety. I can tell you who has the stop authority, what evidence is recorded, and which decisions require another vote.”

Havel tapped the **SAFE** box left empty on the rehearsal sheet.

“Feasible is not safe,” he said.

“Agreed.”

“You continue to make agreement sound like victory.”

“Only when disagreement would be easier.”

The deputy secretary called a recess.

Mara went to the restroom and discovered she had been carrying the same paper cup for two hours. The coffee inside had cooled without being touched. She poured it away and watched the dark liquid move around the drain until there was none left to measure.

When the hearing resumed, the conditions arrived before the decision.

The authorization would cover one sealed, automated construction attempt under the rehearsed custody rules. The output would remain inside the remote enclosure. No animal work. No distribution. No expansion. Independent stop authority for the technicians. Any deviation returned the project to observation only. Further action required another vote.

Havel accepted the conditions because he had written half of them. Grace accepted because the data routes and custody chain were named. Deshpande accepted without qualification.

Davis asked for the words *does nothing* to remain an admissible result.

The deputy secretary added them.

The authorization passed.

Six electronic signatures began routing through document control. Mara watched each status change from pending to complete. Local success arrived as a row of green checks.

No one congratulated her.

That helped.

The final authorization printed at 4:25 in the afternoon behind wired glass.

Jenn read the first page and did not open the containment suite.

The status board above her counted down the reserved instrument window. Forty-one minutes remained before the suite would have to cycle through an overnight reset. Mara stood on the public side of the red line with Deshpande and Havel while the document-control officer waited for Jenn to move.

She did not.

“Page two,” Jenn said.

Mara read it through the glass.

The project title was **CHORUS**.

Every reviewed protocol, custody label and approved screen said **FOUR NOTES**.

The document-control officer took the pages back. “Template field. The authorization number is correct.”

Jenn remained beside the badge reader. “Which project does the number authorize?”

“This project.”

“Then put its name on it.”

“The title is not a controlling field.”

“It controls the label on my door.”

The countdown reached thirty-nine minutes.

Mara imagined the reset, the lost window, the schedules that would have to be rebuilt and the hearing participants who would wake to a delay blamed on typography. She also remembered the authority she had insisted belonged to the person in the room.

“Stop the countdown,” she said.

The control technician stopped it.

The document officer looked at Havel. “We can annotate the correction.”

“Can the annotation be executed inside the approved chain?” Havel asked.

“Yes.”

“Then execute it.”

Deshpande leaned toward the glass. “If the title does not matter, correcting it should not be difficult.”

Jenn looked at Mara. The expression lasted only a moment. Trust was too strong a word. She had tested the new stop authority against the first available inconvenience, and it had held.

Document control traced **CHORUS** to a sterile-process exercise used to build the template. The mistake had no hidden origin and no scientific consequence. Correcting it cost three hours.

The new authorization printed one page at a time. Jenn compared every project identifier before initialling the receipt.

The instrument window was restored for late evening.

Havel stamped the superseded copy **VOID** and left the wrong title visible beneath the ink.

In the parking structure, Mara's missed-call notification slid beneath the containment countdown on her phone.

Forty-eight minutes remained.

She called Tom. The ring continued long enough for her to decide he was asleep. He answered before voicemail.

“Hi,” she said.

“Hi.”

Concrete amplified a cart rolling somewhere below her.

Tom was quiet. “The laboratory that wasn't doing anything.”

“I can't tell you.”

At the secure entrance, the countdown paused until she presented her badge.

The reader turned green. She went inside.

Her phone found a signal after the door closed. Tom's reply appeared on the lock screen she could no longer see.

**Okay. Be safe.**

* * *

Four pipette tips waited above four empty wells.

Every human hand remained outside the robot enclosure.

Jenn stood at the control desk in a fresh gown, the corrected authorization clipped open beside her. Mara, Deshpande and Havel occupied three unequal reflections in the observation glass. Grace watched through a secure video feed whose audio activated only when she spoke.

The suite clock showed 11:38 p.m.

Jenn read the authorization number aloud.

The control technician matched it to the corrected project title. Deshpande confirmed the observation log had begun before any automated action. Havel verified that the technician stop controls responded and that the destruction authority remained available if the process deviated.

Mara read the approved stop conditions one last time. Identifier mismatch. Boundary failure. Unwitnessed handoff. Sensor result outside the validated range. Any person with stop authority could halt the run without justification until the discrepancy was resolved.

Nothing was wrong.

That fact had become the final obstacle.

The robot enclosure held sealed, coded inputs whose preparation belonged to a separate authorized process. Its records could not be opened from this terminal. The control screen showed only custody state, boundary state and permission to continue.

All green.

Jenn rested one finger near her stop control. “Ready.”

Deshpande signed the first observation line. “Ready.”

Havel did not use the word. “No unresolved deviations.”

Grace said, “Authorization active.”

The main control passed to Mara.

On her screen, **ABORT** remained available beside **CONTINUE**.

Observation only was still possible. The broadcast would continue without them. The physical world contained no S-78 sample. Mara could stop and preserve that fact for one more night, perhaps many.

Behind the glass, the robot waited with its four tips raised.

She thought of the map full of pins, the corrected page, Tom asking a question she could not answer. She thought of the empty **SAFE** box and the word **FEASIBLE** stamped above it.

Mara pressed **CONTINUE**.

The status light changed. The robot made a small adjustment over the plate.

Nobody moved.

Four pipettes descended in perfect unison toward four waiting wells.
